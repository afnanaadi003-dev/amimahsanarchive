Synopsis

Feature · Historical epic

The kingdom of Sur is nine years into a war it did not start and cannot finish. Its defence is a single wall, the Long Wall, running from the ridge to the river Dhara; its fortress is Qila Roshan; its king is Arman, who inherited both and would rather be in the archive with his wife.

Queen Mehr keeps the record. She writes down what the council decides and what the council leaves out, and she has noticed that the two are becoming the same thing. When the granary count is forged, when a mason is blamed for a wall he repaired, when the general rides out to burn a camp and does not come back, her ink is the only thing in Sur that does not flatter.

The enemy is Osman of the Karvan, who does not want the kingdom — only the gate, the river, and the road beyond it. He waits for the comet the soldiers call the Witness, which returns every twenty-seven years. He has waited a long time; he can wait three more nights.

A jawla is a ride out and a ride back. The whole film is one, and so is the kingdom.

Across twenty-seven chapters — from the star over the wall to a schoolroom twenty-seven years later — Jawla is a war film told mostly in rooms: a king's chamber, a council table, a granary, an archive, a gate at night. The battles happen off-screen and are described by the people who survive them. What we watch is what it costs to hold a line and to write it down truthfully.

THE STAR OVER THE WALL

4 scenes · 1,016 words · ≈6 min

CameraLocked off, wide, the window slit dividing the frame like a blade; the actors move through the light.
Roll35mm, 160 ISO, cool shadows, no fill — let the dark be dark.
CostumeThe king in a plain grey coat over yesterday's shirt; the general in mail he has not worn in years; the vizier in silk, because silk is a kind of armour.
EnvironmentA long room built inside the thickness of the wall, arrow slits on the east, a table of maps, a brazier, the smell of cold stone.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE LONG WALL — FIRST LIGHT

The wall comes out of the dark the way a sentence comes out of a dream: a line, then an edge, then a fact.

A WATCHMAN, fifty, climbs the stair with a lamp he does not need. He sets it down on the parapet and looks east, and then he stops, because there is a star in the east that should not be there.

It has a tail. A long pale tail, like a brush pulled across wet paper.

The watchman's hand goes to the bell rope. He does not pull it. He stands with his fist around the rope and his mouth open, the way a man stands when he has been given news he has been waiting twenty-seven years to hear and has spent twenty-seven years hoping he would not.

WATCHMAN

(to himself)

The Witness.

The bell begins to ring.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE KING'S ROOM — FIRST LIGHT

KING ARMAN lies on his back with his eyes open. He is forty-six and built like a man who was once useful; the muscle has gone soft over the ribs but the shoulders have not surrendered.

QUEEN MEHR lies beside him, awake, on her side, watching the window and not him.

The bell comes through the stone, muffled, three strokes, then three more.

MEHR

It's the star.

ARMAN

It's a bell.

MEHR

The bell is for the star. The watchman is a romantic.

ARMAN

The watchman is fifty and afraid of the dark.

MEHR

Everyone is afraid of the dark. Some of us have better manners about it.

She turns her head. There is ink on her fingers—the middle two, the way a scribe holds a reed—and a smudge of it on her jaw where she touched her face in her sleep.

ARMAN

You were writing again.

MEHR

I was writing about you.

ARMAN

What did you write?

MEHR

That the king sleeps with his hand open, like a man who has dropped something.

He looks at his hand. It is open. He closes it.

ARMAN

The star comes once in twenty-seven years.

MEHR

I know. My grandmother saw it. She said it made the whole sky look like a room with the door open.

ARMAN

Your grandmother was a poet.

MEHR

She was a fool. They are the same profession.

Outside, the bell stops. In the silence, a horse whinnies in the stable yard, high and frightened, and then another, and then all of them.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE STABLE YARD — SUNRISE

TAMIM, twelve, holds the head of a black stallion, SHABRANG, who is fourteen years old and does not care for the star, the bell, or the boy.

SHABRANG

(which is to say: he stamps, once, hard enough to crack the trough's edge)

TAMIM

Easy. Easy. It's only the sky.

A GROOM, sixty, laughs without warmth.

GROOM

It's only the sky, he says. Boy, the sky has come to collect.

TAMIM

Collect what?

GROOM

Whatever it is owed. Twenty-seven years is a long time to owe.

The stallion turns his head and looks at the gate, and the boy looks with him, because a horse looking at a gate is a kind of prophecy.

The gate is open. Beyond it, on the road, a rider is coming—fast, badly, his horse lathered—and behind him the east is the colour of a peach held up to a lamp, and in it, faint now, the star.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE WAR GALLERY — MORNING

The room is called the War Gallery, and it has not been used for war in nine years. It has been used for storage, for the children's lessons, for a wedding feast, and once, memorably, for a play.

Now the maps are out. GENERAL BAHRAM stands at the eastern slit with a spyglass, though the sun is up and there is nothing to see but the river and the road.

VIZIER NADEEM

(unrolling a paper)

The rider is from the river station. He says the horselords crossed at Anwar ford two nights ago.

ARMAN

How many?

NADEEM

He says four thousand. He is a river clerk. He counts boats, not men. He may be wrong by half.

BAHRAM

(lowering the glass)

He is wrong by half in the wrong direction. Osman does not cross with four. He crosses with eight and lets you count four.

ARMAN

You are certain.

BAHRAM

I am certain of Osman. I have been certain of him since I was twenty and he took my ear.

He turns his head. The left ear is a ruin, folded like a leaf.

ARMAN

That was a long time ago.

BAHRAM

It was the last time we were honest with each other.

Nadeem lays the map flat. On it, the wall is a single line, and beyond the wall the plain is empty and enormous.

NADEEM

If they are at the ford, they will reach the ridge in four days. If they reach the ridge, they will sit at the foot of the wall and eat our harvest and wait for us to be hungry.

ARMAN

And if we ride out?

NADEEM

Then we meet them on the plain, where their horses are better than ours and their numbers are worse than ours.

BAHRAM

We meet them on the plain because a wall is a thing you die behind, and I would rather die in front of it, where at least I can see what killed me.

The king looks at the map for a long time. Then he looks at the slit window, at the light coming through it in a bar, and at the dust turning in the bar.

ARMAN

Send to the villages. Bring them inside the wall. All of them, with their grain and their animals and their old people.

NADEEM

That is nine thousand people, my king.

ARMAN

Then it is nine thousand people who will not be burned in a field.

NADEEM

And the harvest?

ARMAN

Burn it ourselves. Today. Before they can eat it.

Bahram looks at him, and for the first time in years there is something like respect in the ruined face.

BAHRAM

You have been thinking about this.

ARMAN

I have been thinking about it for nine years. I was hoping not to be asked.

THE KING'S ROOM

2 scenes · 625 words · ≈4 min

CameraSlow push in from the doorway, the shelves sliding past the edges of the frame; end on her hands, not her face.
Roll35mm, 320 ISO, the light through a high window falling in a slab on the floor.
CostumeHer robe, now belted; a leather apron over it, stained black at the belly where she leans on the desk.
EnvironmentA room of shelves and scrolls and jars, the smell of gall and gum and dust; a single window, too high to see out of; a ladder on a rail; a cat asleep on a ledger.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE KING'S ROOM — MIDDAY

The queen sits cross-legged on the bed with a board on her knees. On the board, a sheet of paper. She is drawing the star, badly, with a reed pen.

ARMAN

(lying across the foot of the bed, arm over his eyes)

You draw like a woman who has never been taught.

MEHR

I was taught. I was taught by a man who drew like a woman who had never been taught.

ARMAN

Your father.

MEHR

Your father's cartographer. He drew the wall before it was built. He drew it with a garden on the inside and a garden on the outside, and then they built it with a garden only on the inside, and he never drew again.

ARMAN

I remember the drawing. It had a fountain.

MEHR

It had nine fountains. One for each gate.

ARMAN

There are three gates.

MEHR

There were nine. In the drawing.

He takes his arm from his eyes and looks at her. She is not looking at him. She is looking at the star on her paper, which is a smear with a tail.

ARMAN

What is the film of it?

MEHR

The film?

ARMAN

The thing you are making. The picture. What is it for?

MEHR

It is for me. It is an amateur thing. I am not a painter.

ARMAN

You are a queen.

MEHR

That is not a profession, it is a circumstance.

She turns the paper around. The star is bad, and honest, and hers.

MEHR

My grandmother said the star was the eye of something that only opens once in a lifetime. She said if you looked at it and told the truth, it would remember.

ARMAN

And if you lied?

MEHR

Then it would remember that too.

He sits up. The heat moves across his back like a hand.

ARMAN

I am riding in the morning.

MEHR

I know. The whole fort knows. The kitchen knows. The boy in the stable has been crying.

ARMAN

Tamim?

MEHR

He has been crying because he is twelve and because he loves you, in the way boys love a man who once lifted them onto a horse.

ARMAN

I did lift him onto a horse.

MEHR

You lifted him onto Shabrang and Shabrang stood still for a full minute, which for Shabrang is a coronation.

He laughs. It is a rusty sound, rarely used.

Then she says, without looking up:

MEHR

Do not die.

ARMAN

I have no plans.

MEHR

Make some. I am not finished with you.

She goes back to her drawing. He watches her hand, the ink on the fingers, the small precise cruelty of the line.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE INK ROOM — AFTERNOON

The queen keeps the archive of the kingdom: every tax, every wedding, every flood, every war. It is the only room in the fort with a lock on the inside.

She takes down a scroll. Unrolls it on the desk. It is old, and the ink has gone the brown of dried blood.

On it, in a hand that is her grandmother's: THE FIRST JAWLA. THE RIDE OF KING SOHRAB. THE YEAR OF THE STAR.

She reads it the way other people pray: with her lips moving.

MEHR

(reading)

"He rode out at first light with nine hundred men and came back with four hundred and eleven. He said the star watched. He said it did not blink."

She looks up at the high window. The light does not reach her face.

MEHR

(to the room)

Nine hundred. Four hundred and eleven.

She dips her pen. On a fresh sheet she writes: THE SECOND JAWLA. THE RIDE OF KING ARMAN. THE YEAR OF THE STAR.

Then she stops, and puts the pen down, and stands with both hands flat on the desk, and breathes.

THE WAR GALLERY

2 scenes · 773 words · ≈5 min

CameraCrane up from the king's back to the plain, slowly, until the wall is a line at the bottom of the frame and the sky is most of the picture.
Roll35mm, 200 ISO, magic hour, no filter — let the sky do the work.
CostumeThe king's grey coat; the queen's shawl, held at the throat with one hand.
EnvironmentThe parapet at dusk; the plain beyond going violet; the river a wire of light; far off, on the eastern road, the first small fires of an army that has not arrived yet.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE WAR GALLERY — LATE AFTERNOON

Nine men around the table. The Council of Nine, which is seven men and two women, and which has not met since the flood.

NADEEM

The villages are coming in. Four hundred by nightfall, the rest by tomorrow. The granaries are full. The wells are good.

BAHRAM

The wells are good for a month. The granaries are good for two.

NADEEM

Then we have two months.

BAHRAM

Osman has eight thousand men and no harvest. He has one month. If he does not take the wall in one month, his horses starve and his men go home.

ARMAN

Then we do not need to beat him. We need to outlast him.

BAHRAM

Outlasting is a siege. A siege is a wall and a stomach. You are asking eight thousand men to sit in the mud and wait for you to be reasonable.

ARMAN

I am asking them to sit in the mud while their horses eat each other.

A WOMAN AT THE TABLE—the keeper of the granaries, SITARA, fifty, grey-haired, with flour on her sleeve.

SITARA

If they sit, they will eat the countryside. There will be nothing left to come back to.

ARMAN

Then we burn the countryside.

Silence. Nadeem's silk rustles as he shifts.

NADEEM

My king. If we burn the fields, the people will remember the fire, not the reason.

ARMAN

If we do not, the people will remember the horselords.

SITARA

They will remember both. That is what people are for.

The king looks at her. She does not look away. This is why she is on the council.

ARMAN

Burn the fields east of the ridge. Leave the west. Bring the animals inside. Leave the houses—let them have roofs, not food. If Osman wants to winter here, let him winter in an empty kitchen.

BAHRAM

And the wall?

ARMAN

The wall holds.

BAHRAM

The wall is nine metres of stone and it has not been repaired since your father died. The north stretch has a crack you can put your arm in.

ARMAN

Then put a mason in it.

BAHRAM

I would rather put a soldier in it.

ARMAN

Put both. Put a mason and a soldier and a boy with a bucket, and put them there tonight, and do not tell me about the crack again until it is a hole.

He stands. The nine stand.

ARMAN

We ride at first light. Four hundred horse. We do not go to meet him. We go to be seen. We go to the ford and we burn the boats and we come back.

NADEEM

That is a raid, my king, not a war.

ARMAN

It is a jawla. A ride out and a ride back. My father's word for it.

He looks around the table, at the nine faces, and something in his own face closes like a gate.

ARMAN

If I do not come back, the wall is yours. If the wall falls, the fort is yours. If the fort falls—

He stops.

BAHRAM

If the fort falls?

ARMAN

Then there is nothing to bequeath, and we will have been a story. Make it a good one.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE EASTERN PARAPET — DUSK

They stand at the parapet. The wind comes up from the plain, warm and dry, carrying dust and the smell of something burning that is not theirs.

MEHR

You can see their fires.

ARMAN

Those are not their fires. Those are the villages we burned.

MEHR

You can see both.

He says nothing. She wraps her shawl tighter.

MEHR

My grandmother said the star only watches the truth.

ARMAN

Then it will have a dull evening.

MEHR

Do not be clever with me. Be plain.

He turns. In the last light his face is younger and more tired than it was this morning.

ARMAN

I am afraid. I have not held a sword in nine years. I have held a pen and a cup and a child's hand. I do not know if the arm remembers.

MEHR

The arm remembers. It is the rest of you that has been comfortable.

He laughs, once, without pleasure.

ARMAN

You are a hard woman.

MEHR

I am a truthful one. You married me for my dowry and stayed for my cruelty.

ARMAN

I stayed for the ink.

She looks at him. The star is out now, faint, above the eastern fires.

MEHR

Then come back and I will write you a good death.

ARMAN

I would rather have a bad life.

MEHR

Then come back for that. It is cheaper.

She takes his hand. They stand, and the sky goes from violet to grey, and the wall beneath them goes from grey to black.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE STABLE YARD — BEFORE DAWN

Tamim has been awake all night. It shows in the way he holds the brush, in the way he has brushed the same patch of Shabrang's shoulder for an hour.

ARMAN

You will take the hair off him.

TAMIM

He likes it.

ARMAN

He tolerates it. There is a difference, and it is the difference between a horse and a man.

Tamim puts the brush down. The stallion turns his head and blows softly into the boy's hair.

TAMIM

He knows.

ARMAN

He knows everything. He is fourteen. He has outlived two kings and a war.

TAMIM

Will you take him?

ARMAN

I will take him.

TAMIM

He is old.

ARMAN

So am I.

TAMIM

He is old and he has a bad foot in the wet.

ARMAN

Then I will not ride him in the wet.

TAMIM

It is going to rain.

The king looks at the sky. It is not going to rain. It is going to be hot, and then it is going to be very hot, and then, in the afternoon, it will rain, because it always does.

ARMAN

Then he will have a bad foot and I will have a bad temper and we will be even.

He takes the reins. The boy does not let go.

TAMIM

My lord. If you die—

ARMAN

If I die, the horse comes back. He always comes back. He is a coward and a gourmand and he has never once done the noble thing.

TAMIM

That is not true.

ARMAN

It is true. I have ridden him for nine years and he has never once offered to die for me. It is why I trust him.

The boy laughs, and then stops laughing, because the gate is opening and the men are waiting.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE GATE OF DAWN — DAWN

The column goes out slowly, the way all columns go out: not galloping, not glorious, but one horse at a time through a gate that was built for carts.

On the parapet above, the people of Qila Roshan watch. Nine thousand of them, or the half of them who came in from the villages, and their animals, and their children on their shoulders.

Among them, small, at the eastern end where the parapet turns, QUEEN MEHR stands with her hands on the stone.

She does not wave. He does not look up. This is their agreement.

The last rider clears the gate. The gate begins to close, and the light narrows to a line, and the line becomes a thread, and the thread goes out.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE GATE TUNNEL — CONTINUOUS

MEHR

(to the gate)

Come back.

The gate answers with a sound like a held breath let go.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE GATE OF DAWN — DAWN

The gate is three men thick with horses before the sun is properly up.

Arman sits Shabrang at the head of the column. In his father's mail he looks smaller than he does in the hall, and younger, and the men keep glancing at him as if checking the weather.

Bahram rides up beside him without ceremony.

BAHRAM

You will not look at the wall when we go through.

ARMAN

Why not?

BAHRAM

Because they will be on it, and if you look up they will think you are asking permission.

ARMAN

I am asking permission.

BAHRAM

Then ask me. I am closer.

Above them, on the walk, the household has gathered to watch: the cooks, two scribes, the granary-keeper with flour to her elbows. Mehr stands apart from them with her hands on the parapet and does not wave, because a queen waving is a queen afraid.

Tamim holds Shabrang's bridle at the gate mouth. He has not slept; there is straw in his hair.

TAMIM

My lord. The ground beyond the ford is soft. His foot—

ARMAN

I know his foot.

TAMIM

Then you know he will want to go first.

ARMAN

He always wants to go first. It is the only opinion he has.

He reaches down and takes the boy's shoulder, once, the way a man takes a tool he means to give back.

The gate opens. The light comes in flat and white. Shabrang goes through it without being asked, and the column follows, and the dust they raise hangs in the tunnel long after the last of them is out of sight.

ENVOYS IN THE RAIN

4 scenes · 718 words · ≈4 min

CameraHand-held, following the envoys out, then holding on the gate as it closes; the rain arrives in the last second of the shot.
Roll35mm, 640 ISO, blue hour, rain visible only where it crosses the gate light.
CostumeThe envoys' white now grey with wet; the guards' oiled cloaks.
EnvironmentRain on hot stone, steam rising; the road turning to mud in real time; the green branch left behind on the threshold.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE EASTERN ROAD — AFTERNOON

Two riders come up the road at a walk, holding a green branch between them like a rope.

On the wall, the watch bells ring twice: riders, not an army.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE AUDIENCE HALL — LATE AFTERNOON

The elder envoy, HAKIM, sixty, kneels. The younger, his son, does not, until Hakim pulls his sleeve.

HAKIM

From Osman, Sardar of the Karvan, to the king of Sur.

NADEEM

The king is not here.

HAKIM

We know. We passed him on the road. He was going the wrong way.

Nadeem's face does not move. In the corner, the queen's pen stops.

NADEEM

Say what you came to say.

HAKIM

My lord Osman offers terms. The wall, from the eastern ridge to the river. The ford. The three villages of the lower bank. In exchange, he will not cross the river, and the fort will stand, and the king will keep his crown and his city and his queen.

NADEEM

And if we refuse?

HAKIM

Then he will take the wall, and the fort, and the crown, and the city, and the queen—though he says he has no use for the queen, and means it as a courtesy.

The younger envoy smiles. The queen does not look at him.

NADEEM

The king will answer when he returns.

HAKIM

The king will return in three days with fewer men than he left with. My lord Osman will be at the ridge in four. There is no time for a king's answer. There is time for a queen's.

He turns, deliberately, to Mehr.

MEHR

(without looking up)

I am writing.

HAKIM

Then write this: that the offer stands until the star sets.

MEHR

The star sets in twenty-seven years.

HAKIM

Then you have time.

She looks up now. Her face is calm in a way that makes the younger envoy take a step back.

MEHR

You have ridden three days to frighten a woman with astronomy. Tell your sardar that the wall was built by my husband's father, that it has stood through nine floods and two wars, and that it will still be standing when his grandsons are selling their horses for bread.

HAKIM

You are confident.

MEHR

I am a queen. It is the only luxury I allow myself.

EXT. QILA ROSHAN — THE GATE OF DAWN — DUSK

The envoys ride out. The gate closes.

On the threshold, the green branch lies in the dirt. A GUARD goes to pick it up. Nadeem stops him.

NADEEM

Leave it.

GUARD

It will rot.

NADEEM

Then it will rot. Let the people see what we were offered and what we did with it.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE AUDIENCE ROOM — AFTERNOON

Four Karvan riders stand in the middle of the floor and drip onto it. They have been kept standing long enough for the puddle to grow a border.

The eldest holds a rod of painted wood with a cloth wrapped round it, which is how the Karvan send terms: not paper, so nothing can be signed, and nothing kept.

NADEEM

(unrolling his own copy)

He offers the ford and the two villages east of it, and the grain in their granaries, in exchange for the road.

ARMAN

The road through the gate.

NADEEM

The road through the gate.

ARMAN

And in nine years he has not once asked for the kingdom.

NADEEM

He does not want the kingdom. He wants to be able to leave it.

BAHRAM

He wants to be able to come back.

The room is quiet. Outside, the rain turns over on the roof like a hand.

Arman looks at Mehr, who is seated at the side with her tablets on her knees, writing all of it down, including the pause.

ARMAN

My queen. Write this.

MEHR

I am writing.

ARMAN

Write: the wall is not a road. Write: the ford was never mine to give, and the villages are not mine to sell, and the grain belongs to the people who grew it, who are behind that wall because I asked them to be. Write that if he wants the road he may come and take it, and that we will be at the gate when he does.

NADEEM

My king, that is a declaration.

ARMAN

No. A declaration is what you send when you have an army. That is a letter from a man who has a wall.

He nods to the eldest rider.

ARMAN

Tell him I said it exactly.

CameraSlow dolly down the garden path, the camera low enough to brush the flowers; the queen is found, not framed.

EnvironmentA walled garden on the inner face of the wall, night-jasmine in flower, a stone channel of water, a bench, the sky enormous above the parapet.

INT. QILA ROSHAN — THE QUEEN'S GARDEN — NIGHT

The queen walks the garden at midnight with a lamp and a book, and the jasmine is so loud with scent that the air seems to have a colour.

THE COURT POET, SAFI, seventy, sits on the bench in the dark without a lamp, which is how he prefers to be found.

SAFI

You walk like a woman counting.

MEHR

I am counting.

SAFI

How many?

MEHR

Nine hundred.

SAFI

The first jawla.

MEHR

Four hundred and eleven came back.

SAFI

And what will you do with the difference?

She sits beside him. The lamp between them makes a small room of light.

MEHR
