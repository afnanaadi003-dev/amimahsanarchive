CHAPTER 08

The Rajasuya Yagna

The humiliation at Indraprastha's Hall of Illusions burned like lye in Duryodhana's chest, but across the empire, a far grander destiny was unfolding. Yudhishthira, guided by the wisdom of Krishna and backed by the peerless might of his brothers, resolved to perform the Rajasuya Yagna—the ancient, imperial sacrifice that would declare him Samrat, the sovereign emperor of Bharatavarsha.

I. The Conquest of the Four Quarters

To perform the Rajasuya, a monarch must first establish undisputed sovereignty over all surrounding kingdoms, either through voluntary allegiance or victorious war.

The task was divided among the four Pandava brothers:

Bhima marched eastward, subduing powerful rulers such as King Jarasandha of Magadha—whom he defeated in a brutal, bone-crushing fourteen-day wrestling duel to settle an old debt.

Arjuna swept across the wealthy northern territories, bringing immense tribute and securing the submission of mountain kings.

Nakula ventured westward into the arid lands and coastal regions, gathering riches and alliances.

Sahadeva led his forces to the deep south, claiming vast treasures from powerful southern realms.

Within months, the granaries and treasuries of Indraprastha overflowed with gold, jewels, silk, and rare artifacts sent by tributary monarchs from every corner of the earth. The empire was united under a single, righteous umbrella, and preparations for the great sacrifice began.

II. The Imperial Consecration

Kings, sages, priests, and commoners flooded into Indraprastha from all over the world. The smoke of sacred fires filled the air, carrying fragrant offerings to the heavens.

As the rituals commenced, the question of supreme honor arose: Who among the divine assembly should be offered the Arghya—the highest act of ritual worship and precedence?

Yudhishthira turned to his grandfather Bhishma, asking for guidance.

Bhishma did not hesitate. “Offer it to Krishna,” he declared clearly for the entire assembly to hear. “He is the soul of the universe, the sustainer of creation, and the fountainhead of all virtue. None among us equals his glory.”

Yudhishthira immediately followed the counsel, presenting the sacred vessel of honor to Krishna.

III. The Wrath of Shishupala

Across the grand assembly hall, a towering figure rose to his feet, his face twisted in incandescent rage. It was Shishupala, the King of Chedi.

Shishupala had long harbored a deep, venomous hatred for Krishna. Shielded by a maternal boon that decreed he would be invincible until a hundred insults were forgiven by Krishna, Shishupala unleashed a torrent of venomous abuse upon the divine incarnation.

“What madness has seized this assembly?” Shishupala shouted, pointing a shaking finger at Krishna and Yudhishthira. “To honor this cowherd, this trickster from Dwarka, while kings of ancient lineage sit in silence? The House of Kuru has lost its senses!”

The assembly froze. Pandavas reached for their weapons, Bhima rose like a charging bull, and an oppressive silence fell over the hall. Yet Krishna sat completely unperturbed, smiling gently, keeping count of the insults as they tumbled from Shishupala's lips.

One, two, three... up to ninety-nine.

The court held its collective breath. Shishupala, blind to his impending doom, hurled one final, foul insult at Krishna's honor.

The count reached one hundred.

IV. The Sudarshana Chakra

With a serene and absolute calm, Krishna stood up from his seat.

He raised his right hand, and into his palm spun the Sudarshana Chakra—the blazing, razor-edged discus of pure cosmic energy. The assembly hall was instantly illuminated by a light brighter than a thousand suns, and the air hummed with the terrifying vibration of divine judgment.

Before Shishupala could draw his sword, the discus flashed through the air in a flawless, golden arc.

In a single heartbeat, the blade severed Shishupala's head from his shoulders. The Chedi king crashed lifeless to the floor of the assembly hall as the blazing disc returned effortlessly to Krishna's finger, its divine light fading back into quiet peace.

No weapon was drawn by the Pandavas. No chaos erupted. The assembly watched in stunned, breathless reverence as the cosmic order restored itself before their eyes.

Yet, high in the balcony, watching the absolute authority with which Krishna commanded the fate of kings, stood Shakuni and Duryodhana. For them, the Rajasuya Yagna was not a celebration of peace—it was the ultimate proof that as long as Krishna stood beside the Pandavas, they could never be defeated on a fair field.

The dice had to be loaded.

CHAPTER 09

The Loaded Dice

The sacrificial smoke of the Rajasuya Yagna had barely cleared across Bharatavarsha when the dark, retaliatory machinery of Hastinapura began to turn.

Duryodhana returned from Indraprastha like a man poisoned. The memory of his fall into the crystal waters of the Maya Sabha, followed by the stinging laughter of Draupadi, had carved a wound in his pride that time could never heal. He sat in his private chambers, refusing food, his body wracked by bitterness, until his maternal uncle, Shakuni of Gandhara, stepped into the shadows of the room.

I. The Whispers of Gandhara

Shakuni was a man whose mind was as sharp and twisted as the dice carved from his father's skeletal remains. He understood that a direct military assault against the Pandavas—backed by Krishna, allied with powerful kings, and guarded by Arjuna's celestial bow—was suicide.

“You cannot defeat them on the field of battle, my nephew,” Shakuni whispered, leaning over Duryodhana with a thin, cruel smile. “Not with swords, nor with chariots, nor with brute force. But a kingdom can be won without spilling a single drop of royal blood—if you know how to play the game.”

“What game?” Duryodhana spat, looking up with bloodshot eyes.

“The game of dice,” Shakuni replied smoothly. “Yudhishthira has one fatal flaw, a weakness masked as virtue: he loves the play. He considers it a point of honor never to refuse a challenge to cast the dice, no matter the stakes. Leave him to me. I will roll the bones, and I will strip him of his crown, his wealth, and his pride—leaving him naked before the throne, without ever drawing a sword.”

Armed with this malicious plot, Duryodhana approached his blind father, Dhritarashtra. Playing upon the king's deep, blind affection for his own sons and his secret jealousy of the Pandavas' rising glory, Duryodhana and Shakuni poisoned the monarch's mind. Reluctantly, and against the stern warnings of Vidura and Bhishma, Dhritarashtra authorized the construction of a grand assembly hall in Hastinapura and sent an official royal invitation to Yudhishthira, inviting him to a friendly match of dice.

II. The Invitation to the Trap

When the messenger arrived in Indraprastha bearing the invitation from Hastinapura, Yudhishthira received him with traditional royal courtesy.

Vidura, sensing the ominous shadow hanging over the proposal, hurried to Indraprastha in secret to warn the Pandava king. “Do not go, Yudhishthira,” Vidura pleaded quietly. “The dice of Hastinapura are not carved of ivory or wood; they are instruments of destruction. Shakuni's hand is in this, and destruction walks behind his smile.”

Yudhishthira listened with respect, yet his strict adherence to the code of the Kshatriya trapped him. “I know the danger, Uncle Vidura,” Yudhishthira replied with solemn resolve. “Yet if a king is challenged to a game of chance by his elders and peers, honor dictates that he cannot refuse. To turn away out of fear is a cowardice worse than death. Whatever comes, I must obey the path of duty.”

With heavy hearts, Yudhishthira, his brothers, and Queen Draupadi set out for Hastinapura, walking directly into the lion's den.

III. The Rigged Board

The grand assembly hall of Hastinapura was packed with the elders of the Kuru court. Bhishma sat with his head bowed in sorrow; Dronacharya and Kripacharya looked on with heavy foreboding; Vidura stood near a pillar, his arms crossed in silent protest. At the center of the hall sat Dhritarashtra, while Duryodhana looked on with a triumphant, predatory smirk.

Across the checkered board sat Shakuni, holding the dice.

“Shall we begin, Dharmaraja?” Shakuni purred, his eyes gleaming with malicious intent. “Name your first stake.”

Yudhishthira, trusting in fairness, smiled calmly. “Let the game commence.”

From the very first throw, the board was cursed. Shakuni's dice possessed a supernatural life of their own, dancing across the inlaid board to obey his silent will, while Yudhishthira's rolls crumbled into defeat.

“Lost,” Shakuni murmured cheerfully, as the pieces fell again. “Another loss, my king.”

Fascinated by the challenge and blinded by the hypnotic roll of the ivory, Yudhishthira's competitive spirit turned into a desperate fever. As losses mounted, his pride and his desperate desire to regain his footing blinded him to the precipice beneath his feet.

“I stake my gold and silver treasuries,” Yudhishthira called out, his voice tightening. Lost. “I stake my chariots, my horses, and my royal garments.” Lost. “I stake my kingdom, my cities, and all the lands of Indraprastha.” Lost.

A collective gasp rippled through the assembly hall. Bhishma closed his eyes in agony, unable to watch the sovereign emperor of Bharatavarsha gamble away an empire on the flick of a rigged die. Vidura stepped forward, raising his voice in absolute condemnation. “Stop this madness, O King! This is not a game; this is a slaughter of our own blood!”

Duryodhana rounded on Vidura with bitter fury, shouting him down, while Dhritarashtra remained silent, his heart secretly rejoicing as the Pandavas' empire vanished into his son's hands.

IV. Staking the Blood

Stripped of his kingdom, his wealth, and his crown, Yudhishthira stood before the board pale, sweating, and psychologically unmoored. He was no longer playing to win; he was playing to reverse the crushing humiliation of defeat.

Shakuni leaned across the table, his voice dripping with venomous amusement. “You have lost everything, Yudhishthira. Your treasury is empty, your lands belong to Duryodhana. Surely, as a king, you still possess something of value to stake?”

Yudhishthira's eyes darted across the hall to where his brothers sat in grim, silent loyalty. Bound by their duty to the eldest brother and the strict laws of the game, they could not interfere.

“I stake Nakula,” Yudhishthira whispered, the words tearing from his throat. “My brother is worth more than all the gold in the world. Win him, and he is yours.”

The dice rolled. Lost.

“Sahadeva!” Yudhishthira shouted, his composure completely fracturing. Lost.

“Arjuna—the invincible archer, my brother!” Lost.

“Bhima—whose strength is the shield of our house!” Lost.

One by one, the mighty Pandava brothers—demigods among men, heroes of the Rajasuya Yagna—were staked and lost to Shakuni's magical dice, reduced to chattel in the center of the Kuru court.

Yet Yudhishthira's fever did not break. Driven by a hollow, desperate compulsion to reclaim what he had thrown away, he stared at the board with bloodshot eyes.

“I have one left,” Yudhishthira breathed, his voice trembling. “Myself. I stake Yudhishthira.”

The entire assembly held its collective breath. The dice rolled across the inlaid floor, spun, and came to rest.

“Lost,” Shakuni whispered, his smile stretching wide across his face.

Yudhishthira sat frozen. He was no longer a king, no longer a free man, no longer even master of his own breath. He belonged to Duryodhana.

Yet Shakuni, tasting absolute victory, leaned forward once more, whispering the final, fatal temptation that would drag the entire house of Kuru into eternal damnation.

“Not quite everything, Yudhishthira,” Shakuni hissed softly. “You still have Draupadi, the queen of Indraprastha. Stake her. If you win, you win back everything you have lost.”

CHAPTER 10

The Shaming of the Queen

The air in the grand assembly hall of Hastinapura had grown thick, stagnant with the stench of male triumph and the suffocating silence of cowards.

Yudhishthira stood paralyzed near the checkered board, a man hollowed out by his own compulsive addiction to dharma turned obsession. He had staked his wealth, his kingdom, his four brothers, and finally himself—losing every single wager to Shakuni's rigged dice.

Yet Shakuni, leaning forward with a cruel, triumphant glint in his eye, whispered the final, unspeakable stroke of malice. “You still have Draupadi, Yudhishthira. Stake her. If you win, you win back everything.”

Without a word of protest—trapped by the rigid, self-destructive logic of a gambler who believes the next roll will reverse his ruin—Yudhishthira spoke the fatal words.

“I stake Draupadi.”

I. The Summons of the Queen

A collective shudder rippled through the Kuru elders. Bhishma closed his eyes in profound agony, his head dropping against his chest. Dronacharya looked away, his face pale with shame. Vidura pressed his knuckles to his mouth, weeping silent tears for a kingdom that had already lost its soul.

Duryodhana broke into a harsh, victorious laugh and turned to his personal attendant, Pratikami. “Go to the inner chambers,” Duryodhana commanded, his voice dripping with malice. “Bring Draupadi here. Let her witness that her husbands are now our slaves. Let her sit among us.”

Pratikami hurried to the royal apartments where Draupadi sat resting. Trembling, the messenger delivered the horrifying news: Yudhishthira had lost everything in the game of dice, and she was now the property of the Kauravas.

Draupadi's dark eyes flashed with fire. She stood tall, her royal bearing unbowed by the shock. “Go back to those gamblers,” she demanded with fierce clarity, “and ask the one question every man in that hall is too cowardly to utter: Did Yudhishthira stake himself first, or did he stake me after he had already lost his own freedom? If a man has lost himself, he owns nothing more—not even his wife. Go and ask the assembly whose property I am!”

Pratikami returned to the hall and repeated her words.

Instead of addressing the profound legal and moral truth of her question, Duryodhana panicked at the thought of losing his prize. Turning to his fierce and brutal younger brother, Dushasana, he snarled, “She questions the assembly? Go and drag her here by her hair. Let her ask her questions to our faces!”

II. Dragged into the Court

Dushasana did not hesitate. Striding into the inner chambers with heavy, insolent steps, he found Draupadi alone. When she tried to retreat, Dushasana reached out, seized her by her long, dark, fragrant hair, and ripped her away from the sanctuary of her chambers.

Draupadi cried out in protest, her cries echoing through the marble corridors as she was dragged—unprotected, half-veiled, and weeping—across the courtyards and into the grand assembly hall.

The sight of the queen of Indraprastha, the daughter of Panchala, hauled by her hair across the floor of the Kuru court struck the assembly like a physical blow. The Pandavas sat bound by their word, their faces twisted in murderous, impotent fury—veins bulging on their foreheads, ready to tear the hall apart if only a single command were given.

Dushasana shoved her forward so that she stumbled before the throne. “Dance for your masters, queen!” Dushasana mocked, laughing crudely. “You belong to Duryodhana now!”

Draupadi wiped the dust from her brow, standing upright despite her humiliation. She looked across the sea of faces, searching for a single spark of honor.

She looked upon Bhishma, the grand patriarch, who sat with his head bowed so low his beard touched his chest, murmuring only, “Dharma is subtle, my child... I cannot unravel it.” She looked upon Dronacharya, her martial preceptor, who stared rigidly at the floor. She looked upon her husbands—the mighty demigods—who sat with their heads hung low, paralyzed by their rigid adherence to a code that had become a prison.

There was no justice in the hall. Only wolves.

III. The Attempt to Disrobe

Sensing that the elders were too paralyzed to intervene, Duryodhana decided to break the Pandavas completely by breaking their queen. He patted his thigh, looking at Draupadi with a leering, arrogant grin. “Come, Panchali! Sit upon the lap of your master. Your husbands have lost you to me.”

A collective gasp of absolute horror echoed through the hall. Even among the Kauravas, a few lowered their heads in shame—except for Vikarna, who stood up and shouted that the game was rigged and the queen was not lawfully won, though his protests were quickly drowned out by Shakuni's faction.

Draupadi stood tall, her voice ringing out with the terrifying authority of a wronged goddess. “You are blind fools, every one of you! A man who has lost himself has no right to wager another!”

Enraged by her defiance, Duryodhana nodded to Dushasana. “She resists. Strip her of her garments, Dushasana. Let her stand naked before the court of her new masters!”

Dushasana stepped forward. Seizing the edge of Draupadi's fine silken sari, he yanked it violently.

Draupadi closed her eyes. She knew her husbands could not help her. She knew the elders were cowards wrapped in tradition. In that ultimate moment of absolute helplessness, when every human shield had dissolved, she lifted both her arms toward the heavens, surrendered her pride, and cried out with every ounce of her soul to the only one who transcended the corrupted laws of men—Krishna.

IV. The Endless Veil

Dushasana pulled. The silk yielded.

He yanked the fabric again, expecting her to collapse in shame. But as the first length of cloth unwound, another appeared beneath it. He pulled harder. Another layer unfurled.

Sweat poured down Dushasana's face. He spun in circles, frantically unravelling yard after yard of rich, divine cloth that cascaded across the marble floor in an endless, shimmering wave of color and light. The hall fell into a stunned, terrifying hush. The pile of silk grew higher and higher, burying Dushasana's feet, while Draupadi stood untouched, glowing with a sovereign, untouchable light, wrapped in the infinite grace of the divine.

Exhausted, humiliated, and trembling with supernatural terror, Dushasana finally collapsed backward onto the floor, letting go of the fabric as his arms grew too weak to lift.

V. The Vow of Fire

A heavy, oppressive silence settled over the assembly hall. Even Duryodhana's arrogance faltered before the unmistakable sign of divine wrath.

From the back of the hall, Bhima stood up. His voice did not shake; it rumbled like an approaching earthquake that shook the foundations of Hastinapura.

“Hear me, O elders of Kuru, and hear me, all kings of the earth,” Bhima roared, his eyes blazing with incandescent fury. “If I do not rip open Dushasana's chest and drink his warm blood on the battlefield, may I never attain the ancestors' heaven! And if I do not shatter Duryodhana's thighs with my mace before this war is done, may I be branded an outcast!”

Arjuna, Yudhishthira, and the twins stood beside him, their silent fury echoing the vow of death.

Before the panic could fully seize the Kuru court, a sudden, violent omen struck the palace. Jackals howled outside the grand gates, crows cawed inauspiciously from the roof beams, and flashes of lightning split the clear sky.

Fearing total annihilation and desperate to avert immediate destruction, the blind king Dhritarashtra intervened. Trembling, he turned to Draupadi. “Daughter, you are the most noble of our house. Ask of me whatever boon you desire, and it shall be granted.”

Draupadi did not ask for wealth or kingdoms. She looked straight at the blind king. “Release my husbands. Free Yudhishthira and my lords from bondage. Let them walk out of this hall as free men.”

Dhritarashtra immediately granted her wish. The chains of slavery were broken, the stakes were reversed, and the Pandavas, holding their heads high, turned their backs on Hastinapura to return to Indraprastha.

Yet the board had been cleared, the honor had been stripped, and the vows of blood had been spoken. The dice game was over, but the war had already begun.
