CHAPTER 03

The Five Divine Sons

High above the dusty plains where the towers of Hastinapura pierced the clouds, the southern slopes of the Himalayas offered a silence so deep it felt almost sacred. Here, among towering deodars, rushing glacial streams, and hermitages draped in morning mist, King Pandu walked not as a conqueror of realms, but as a penitent stripped of his crown.

His two queens, Kunti and Madri, walked beside him in simple garments of bark and unbleached linen. They had traded the silk canopies and golden chariots of the capital for the austerity of the forest without a single word of complaint. Yet beneath the tranquil beauty of Shatashringa—the Mountain of a Hundred Peaks—an invisible wall stood between the exiled monarch and his wives, built from blood, sorrow, and an unbreakable curse.

I. The Arrow in the Woods and the Weight of the Curse

Years earlier, during a royal hunt in the dense foothills, Pandu had sighted two deer coupled together in a sunlit clearing. Driven by a hunter's instinct, the king drew his bow and loosed five sharp arrows, striking both creatures down.

As he approached his quarry, the forest air shifted with an eerie chill. The dying stag lifted its head, and from its throat emerged not the cry of a beast, but the resonant, agonized voice of a man. He was Sage Kindama, an ascetic who had assumed the form of a deer alongside his wife so they might know earthly intimacy free from human intrusion.

With blood staining the moss beneath him, Kindama fixed his glazing eyes upon the horrified monarch. “Even the beasts of the wilderness are spared while they unite, O King,” the sage gasped, his breath rattling in his chest. “Yet you, born of a noble lineage, struck us down without mercy. Hear my final word: you too shall share my fate. The very instant you embrace either of your wives with desire, death shall claim you on the spot.”

The curse fell upon Pandu like a thunderbolt. Returning to Hastinapura shattered by remorse, he renounced his sovereignty, placed the administration of the empire into the hands of Dhritarashtra, Bhishma, and Vidura, and retreated to the Himalayas to wash away his sin through strict asceticism.

In the quiet of Shatashringa, the sages welcomed Pandu with warmth, praising his humility. Yet as seasons turned and snow melted into spring, a profound grief began to hollow out the exiled king. One evening, sitting beside a mountain stream as twilight painted the peaks in shades of violet and ash, Pandu looked at Kunti and spoke the truth that haunted his waking hours.

“A man without sons,” Pandu whispered, staring into the dark water, “is bound by debts he can never repay to his ancestors, to the gods, and to the earth itself. Through my own rash folly, I have severed my branch of the Kuru tree. My life in these woods is barren, Kunti, for I can never give you the joy of motherhood, nor leave behind heirs to carry my name.”

Kunti listened in silence, her heart aching for the husband who bore his penance with such dignity. She remembered the elders' teachings on dharma—that when a husband is unable to father children, a wife may, with his explicit blessing, bring forth progeny through a sacred, sanctioned union. More than that, she remembered a secret locked deep within her youth—a divine gift she had never spoken of since entering the Kuru household.

II. The Secret of Durvasa and the Birth of Dharmaraja

Years before her marriage, when Kunti was still a young princess in the palace of her foster father, King Kuntibhoja, she had served the volatile and famously short-tempered Sage Durvasa with unmatched patience and devotion. Pleased beyond measure by her grace and selflessness, Durvasa had bestowed upon her a sacred mantra from the Atharva Veda.

“Whichever celestial deity you invoke with this sacred verse,” the sage had told her, “shall be compelled to appear before you and grant you a son endowed with his own divine splendor and power.”

In the innocent curiosity of adolescence, Kunti had once tested the mantra upon Surya, the Sun God—a secret union that had resulted in the birth of Karna, whom she had tearfully set adrift in a basket upon the river out of terror and shame. She had sworn never to utter the sacred words again.

Now, seeing Pandu crushed beneath the weight of childlessness, Kunti bowed her head and revealed the existence of Durvasa's boon—keeping only her youthful mistake hidden in the shadows of her heart.

Pandu's eyes lit up with a sudden, radiant hope. Taking Kunti's hands in his, he urged her without hesitation. “This is the grace of heaven itself, Kunti! Invoke the gods according to dharma, so that our lineage may live on. First, call upon Dharma—the Lord of Justice and Cosmic Law—so that our eldest son may never stray from the path of righteousness.”

On a sacred lunar day, purified by fasting and prayer, Kunti stood in a secluded grove bathed in golden morning light and recited the ancient mantra.

The sky above Shatashringa shimmered, and Dharma himself descended in a chariot of pure radiance, smiling gently upon the devoted queen. By his divine blessing, Kunti conceived, and at the auspicious hour when the moon stood in alignment with the constellation Jyeshtha, she gave birth to a son whose infant face shone with serene wisdom.

As the child lay wrapped in soft linen, an incorporeal voice echoed from the heavens across the mountain peaks: “This child shall be the foremost among the upholders of truth and duty. Unshakable in righteousness and steady in battle, he shall be known throughout the three worlds as Yudhishthira.”

III. Wind, Thunder, and the Twin Stars

Joy returned to the hermitage of Shatashringa, yet Pandu knew that a single heir was fragile in a world of shifting alliances and royal rivalries. “A ruler requires strength to protect righteousness,” Pandu told Kunti. “Invoke Vayu, the Lord of the Wind, that we may be blessed with a son of unmatched physical might.”

Kunti chanted the sacred verse once more. The mountain pines bent low before a mighty, fragrant gale, and Vayu manifested in dazzling form, granting her a second son.

Not long after his birth, a terrifying incident revealed the child's staggering power. Startled by a roaring tiger while holding the infant near a steep precipice, Kunti accidentally dropped the baby onto the jagged boulders below. She cried out in horror, rushing down the slope—only to find the child laughing playfully amidst a shattered heap of stone. His infant body had crushed the mountain rock into dust without suffering a single scratch.

Thus was Bhima named—the Terrible and Mighty—destined to possess the strength of ten thousand elephants and a heart as fierce as a storm.

Still, Pandu looked toward the future and desired a third son who would embody supreme skill, nobility, and martial mastery. Together, Pandu and Kunti observed an entire year of rigorous austerities to please Indra, the King of Heaven. At the year's end, Indra appeared amidst rolling thunder and flashes of celestial lightning, promising Kunti a son whose glory would eclipse that of every warrior on earth.

When the third boy was born, celestial music filled the skies of Shatashringa, flowers rained down upon the hermitage, and sages gathered from across the Himalayas to behold him. He was named Arjuna—the Bright and Pure—destined to become the peerless archer of his age.

Watching Kunti surrounded by three radiant, god-born sons, Madri felt a quiet, aching sorrow settle over her heart. Though Kunti treated her with sisterly affection, Madri longed to cradle children of her own blood. When she confessed her grief to Pandu, the king approached Kunti gently, asking if she would share the divine mantra with Madri so that both queens might know fulfillment.

True to her generous spirit, Kunti agreed without hesitation, instructing Madri in the sacred invocation. Madri turned her thoughts toward the Ashvini Kumaras, the divine twin physicians of the heavens renowned for their beauty, grace, and healing wisdom.

The radiant twins descended together, blessing Madri with two sons at once: Nakula and Sahadeva, boys of extraordinary beauty, sharp intellect, and gentle, devoted hearts.

IV. The Spring Breeze and the Fatal Embrace

For several years, the slopes of Shatashringa echoed with the laughter of the five Pandavas. Pandu walked among his sons with a swelling pride that healed the bitterness of his exile. Though born of divine intervention, the five boys loved Pandu as their true father, and the sages of the forest nurtured them in the Vedas, archery, and the sacred duties of kingship.

Yet a curse patiently awaits its hour.

One afternoon in the height of spring, the Himalayan forest burst into sudden, intoxicating bloom. Warm breezes carried the heavy scent of wild jasmine and lotus blossoms across the valleys, and the air hummed with the calls of mating birds. Pandu walked alone through a secluded grove when he came upon Madri, draped in a thin, flowing garment that clung gently to her frame as she gathered wildflowers near a stream.

For a single, fatal heartbeat, the ascetic discipline Pandu had maintained for years dissolved. Overwhelmed by spring's enchantment and the beauty of his young queen, Pandu forgot Sage Kindama's warning. Despite Madri's panicked cries and desperate attempts to pull away, the king swept her into his arms.

The curse struck instantaneously. A sharp, agonizing pain pierced Pandu's chest, his breath failed, and the mighty monarch of the Kuru race collapsed lifeless onto the flower-strewn earth.

Madri's piercing wails brought Kunti and the five young boys running through the trees. Seeing Pandu lying still and cold upon the grass, Kunti fell beside him in shattering grief. When she learned how the tragedy had unfolded, Kunti cried out in anguish—yet Madri, consumed by unbearable sorrow and guilt for having been the instrument of her husband's death, refused to remain behind.

“You are wiser and stronger than I, elder sister,” Madri wept, embracing Kunti and kissing the foreheads of her twin sons one final time. “Raise my Nakula and Sahadeva alongside your own three sons. I cannot bear to live while my lord journeys alone into the dark.”

Before Kunti or the sages could stay her hand, Madri ascended Pandu's blazing funeral pyre, joining her husband in death.

When the flames finally died down to gray ash upon the mountain peak, Kunti stood alone with five young boys—Yudhishthira barely sixteen, the twins still children—clinging to her garments. The sages of Shatashringa gathered around the bereaved queen and made a solemn decision: these five divine sons did not belong to the wilderness. They were princes of the Kuru bloodline, and their rightful place was within the walls of Hastinapura.

Leading Kunti and the five boys down from the icy heights toward the distant plains, the ascetics prepared to present Pandu's heirs to the blind king Dhritarashtra—unaware that the gates of Hastinapura were opening not to welcome five orphans, but to ignite a rivalry that would consume an empire.

CHAPTER 04

The Poisoned Apples

The mountain winds of Shatashringa were left far behind when Kunti and her five sons finally descended into the plains of Hastinapura. To the waiting citizens, the arrival of the five orphaned princes felt like a long-awaited blessing. They were tall, radiant, and imbued with the quiet grace of their celestial fathers. Bhishma welcomed them with tears in his eyes, mourning his nephew Pandu, while Vidura embraced them as a loving uncle, sensing both the light they brought and the darkness they were walking into.

Yet, within the marble halls of the palace, not everyone rejoiced.

I. The Shadow in the Nursery

For years, Duryodhana had grown up as the undisputed eldest prince of Hastinapura, the heir apparent to a throne that his blind father had reluctantly occupied. Surrounded by flatterers, Shakuni's whispers, and his ninety-nine younger brothers, Duryodhana viewed his status as an absolute right.

The arrival of the Pandavas shattered that illusion overnight.

Though Yudhishthira was older and possessed a quiet, natural dignity that drew courtiers and commoners alike, it was Bhima who became the immediate target of Duryodhana's burning envy. Unfiltered, immensely strong, and fiercely competitive, young Bhima dominated the palace courtyards. He would effortlessly toss his Kaurava cousins into the river during swimming games, bend iron bows with a casual laugh, and outperform them in every martial sport.

Duryodhana's pride could not stomach it. He watched the Pandavas with eyes narrowed in hatred, whispering to his maternal uncle, Shakuni—the cunning prince of Gandhara who had taken up residence in Hastinapura to guide his sister's sons.

“As long as those five live,” Shakuni murmured into Duryodhana's receptive ear, “the crown will never truly be yours, my nephew. A tiger does not share its den with wolves. They must be uprooted before their roots sink too deep into the soil of Hastinapura.”

II. The Poisoned Cup

The first attempt was designed to look like the cruel work of chance.

During a grand festival along the banks of the Ganga, the royal household organized games and a massive feast. Duryodhana, wearing a mask of false warmth, approached Bhima with sweetmeats and cups of rich, spiced fruit juice laced with a potent, paralyzing neurotoxin—a poison concocted in the dark laboratories of Gandhara that could fell an elephant within minutes.

Bhima, suspecting nothing and possessing an endless appetite, drank deeply.

As afternoon turned to evening, the heavy poison took hold. Bhima's limbs grew leaden, his vision blurred, and he collapsed unconscious upon the riverbank. Smiling with cold satisfaction, Duryodhana and his brothers bound the limp body tightly with thick creepers, dragged it to the edge of the river, and rolled it into the deep, dark currents of the Ganga, watching it sink beneath the swirling water.

They returned to the palace believing their secret was safe, celebrating the removal of the strongest obstacle in their path.

Yet fate had other designs. As Bhima sank into the abyss, his body drifted into the subterranean realm of the Nagas—the serpent kings. The toxic venom in his veins reacted violently with the powerful nectars of the serpent realm. Awakened by the disturbance, the serpent king Vasuki and his council examined the unconscious youth. Recognizing him as the son of Vayu and the nephew of Kunti, they poured potent antidotes down his throat and fed him elixirs of boundless strength.

Three days later, Bhima awoke. He did not merely survive; he crawled back out of the river endowed with the strength of a thousand serpents, returning to the palace halls to the utter horror and disbelief of Duryodhana.

III. The House of Lac (Lakshagriha)

When poison failed, Shakuni and Duryodhana turned to a more elaborate, architectural treachery.

Realizing that open violence would draw the wrath of Bhishma and Vidura, they devised a slow, calculated trap. At Duryodhana's urging, a grand festival of worship was proclaimed in the outlying town of Varanavata. The Kuru elders, deceived by the sudden generosity and apparent affection shown by Duryodhana toward his cousins, permitted the Pandavas and Kunti to travel there for a prolonged holiday.

Unknown to them, Duryodhana had commissioned his master architect, Purochana, to construct a luxurious palace for the family. But this was no ordinary dwelling. The entire palace—walls, pillars, floors, and ceiling beams—was built of Laksha (lac), hemp, resin, turpentine, cow fat, and other highly combustible materials, mixed meticulously to look like ordinary stone and polished wood.

The plan was simple: once the Pandavas were settled and asleep inside, Purochana was to set fire to the palace from the outside, burning them alive so that their deaths would appear to be a tragic, accidental fire.

As the Pandavas entered the palace of lac, the oppressive smell of resin hung faintly in the air, unnoticed by most. But Vidura, whose sharp vigilance missed nothing, had grown deeply suspicious of Duryodhana's sudden hospitality. Before the family departed for Varanavata, Vidura had met the Pandavas in secret, speaking to them in a coded dialect of ancient miners.

“The fire that consumes dry grass cannot harm a burrowing animal,” Vidura had warned Yudhishthira quietly. “He who understands the hidden tunnels beneath the earth survives the night.”

IV. Escape Through the Earth

Arriving in Varanavata, Yudhishthira understood the warning immediately. He placed his trusted brothers on high alert.

True to Vidura's foresight, a trusted miner sent by the wise minister arrived in secret disguise, spending nights digging a deep, underground tunnel that led from the center of the palace chambers out into the dense, dark forest beyond the town walls.

For months, the Pandavas lived in the shimmering, beautiful palace of lac, pretending ignorance while waiting for the trap to spring.

The night arrived during a heavy monsoon storm. Purochana, believing the family fast asleep after a night of feasting, bolted the heavy outer gates of the courtyard from the outside and set fire to the grand entrance. Within moments, the volatile resin caught the flame, and the palace erupted into an inferno of blinding orange light and suffocating smoke.

Purochana stood outside, smiling as he watched the building burn to the ground, certain that the Pandavas had perished in the blaze.

Yet beneath the burning floors, through the dark, damp earth of Vidura's secret tunnel, Kunti and her five sons crawled silently away from the flames, vanishing into the wilderness of the night. To ensure the world believed them dead, they left behind the charred remains of a forest-dwelling woman and her five sons who had accidentally sought shelter in the palace that very night.

When the news reached Hastinapura, the court plunged into mourning. Dhritarashtra wept theatrical tears, while Duryodhana suppressed a triumphant smile.

Yet the Pandavas were not dead. They were walking the dark paths of exile, shedding their princely robes for the rags of ascetics, learning the hard lessons of survival—knowing that the day of reckoning had only been delayed, not denied.
