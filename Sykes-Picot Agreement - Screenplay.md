# SYKES–PICOT

*A historical screenplay in 42 chapters.*

In a small room in London, two diplomats divide an empire that is not theirs. What follows is not the history of a treaty but of the people who grew up inside it — a Damascus merchant, his daughter, her granddaughter, and the line they cannot stop explaining.

Salim, Nadia and Lina Haddad are fictional. The events around them are not.

---

## CHAPTER 01 — THE COMMITTEE

*Act I · CABINET ROOM, WHITEHALL · DECEMBER 1915*

**INT. CABINET ROOM, WHITEHALL — 16 DECEMBER 1915**

A map of the Ottoman Empire is pinned over the fireplace, its corners curling in the heat. Someone has already pencilled a line across it and rubbed it out twice.

SIR MARK SYKES (37) is rumpled, ink on his cuff, standing because sitting makes him feel like a civil servant. SIR MAURICE DE BUNSEN (60) chairs. A COLONEL from the War Office holds a pipe he has not lit.

**DE BUNSEN**

The Prime Minister wants options. Not a settlement. Options.

**SYKES**

The war will settle it whether we do or not. Every week we wait, someone else draws a line.

**COLONEL**

The French have already drawn one.

**SYKES**

Then we shall draw faster.

He pulls the map flat. The Ottoman provinces look enormous and empty, the way other people's countries always do.

**DE BUNSEN**

And the Arabs? The Sharif has been writing to Cairo.

**SYKES**

The Sharif writes poetry. The French write memoranda. Memoranda win.

**DE BUNSEN**

(tired) You have three weeks.

Sykes takes out a pencil. Before he touches the map he stops.

**SYKES**

We are not dividing a country. We are drafting the will of a man who is not dead yet.

**DE BUNSEN**

Then write it so the family does not fight.

Sykes looks at the map for a long time. Then he draws.

## CHAPTER 02 — THE SHARIF

*Act I · BRITISH RESIDENCY, CAIRO · OCTOBER 1915*

**INT. BRITISH RESIDENCY, CAIRO — OCTOBER 1915**

Ceiling fans. A tray of untouched coffee. SIR HENRY MCMAHON (52) reads a translation while RONALD STORRS (28) stands at the window, watching the street.

**STORRS**

He asks for the whole coast. Syria, Iraq, the holy places. Everything the Turks hold in Arabia.

**MCMAHON**

And in return he offers a revolt we cannot afford to refuse.

**STORRS**

And the French?

**MCMAHON**

The French are not in this room.

Storrs turns. On the desk, the letter's key sentence has been underlined twice: the districts of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo.

**STORRS**

Those four words. They are a door left open in a wall we are promising to build.

**MCMAHON**

Then we leave the door.

**STORRS**

Sir. Do we tell him what we mean?

**MCMAHON**

We tell him what we can defend in a cabinet room in London.

He signs. The pen scratches. Outside, a muezzin starts, and Storrs listens to it the way a man listens to a language he will never speak well.

**STORRS**

He will remember this letter.

**MCMAHON**

Everyone remembers the letter. Nobody remembers the map.

## CHAPTER 03 — THE QUAI

*Act I · QUAI D'ORSAY, PARIS · NOVEMBER 1915*

**INT. QUAI D'ORSAY, PARIS — NOVEMBER 1915**

High windows, grey light, the smell of paper and cold ash. FRANÇOIS GEORGES-PICOT (43) is neat, quick, and entirely certain. PAUL CAMBON (74) sits with his coat still on.

**PICOT**

Syria is ours. Not because we want it. Because if we do not take it, the British will give it to someone who will.

**CAMBON**

And Mosul?

**PICOT**

Mosul is wheat, oil, and a road to nothing. We will take it anyway. It makes the map look serious.

**CAMBON**

The British will want Palestine.

**PICOT**

Let them have the desert and the Jews and the expense.

Cambon looks at him over his glasses. Picot does not notice. He is already drawing: a blue sweep down the coast, a red block to the east, a brown patch in the middle.

**CAMBON**

You sound like a man who has already won.

**PICOT**

I sound like a man who has read the British.

He holds up the map. The blue reaches almost to the desert.

**PICOT (CONT'D)**

This is not a conquest. It is a claim filed before the fire.

**CAMBON**

And the people in the building?

**PICOT**

They will be French. They will thank us eventually.

## CHAPTER 04 — THE LINE

*Act I · FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON · DECEMBER 1915*

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, MAP ROOM — DECEMBER 1915**

Sykes works at a table covered in maps, atlases, and one sheet of tracing paper. A JUNIOR CLERK (22) holds a ruler like a rifle.

**SYKES**

Have you been to the Levant?

**CLERK**

No, sir.

**SYKES**

Nor have I. That is the beauty of it. We are arguing about places we cannot pronounce.

He sets the ruler down at Acre and draws east, past the Sea of Galilee, across the desert, up toward Kirkuk. The pencil goes blunt halfway.

**SYKES (CONT'D)**

Acre to Kirkuk. One line. It gives the French the coast, us the desert, and it puts a wall between us that neither of us has to man.

**CLERK**

And the people between?

**SYKES**

They will be the wall.

He stops. The pencil hovers.

**SYKES (CONT'D)**

Bring me a map with the tribes on it.

**CLERK**

We don't have one, sir.

**SYKES**

Then bring me a map that doesn't.

The clerk goes. Sykes looks at the line he has made and, for a moment, looks like a man who has seen the future and finds it badly drawn.

## CHAPTER 05 — THE ZONE

*Act I · HOTEL MEURICE, PARIS · JANUARY 1916*

**INT. HOTEL MEURICE, PARIS — JANUARY 1916**

Picot's counter-map covers the table: blue for France, red for Britain, brown for Palestine. Sykes studies the brown.

**PICOT**

An international condominium. A committee. A beautiful, endless committee.

**SYKES**

The Zionists will not accept a committee.

**PICOT**

The Zionists are not a great power.

**SYKES**

(mild) Not yet.

Picot's finger moves north, to Mosul.

**PICOT (CONT'D)**

And this is French.

**SYKES**

You cannot hold Mosul from Beirut. It is four hundred miles of nothing.

**PICOT**

We held it from Paris for four hundred years.

**SYKES**

Through us.

A pause. Picot smiles without warmth.

**PICOT**

Then you will hold it for us again, and we will call it gratitude.

Sykes takes the blue pencil and, deliberately, moves the border at Mosul a half-inch south. Picot's smile goes away.

**SYKES**

Half an inch. Nobody will die over half an inch.

**PICOT**

Everybody dies over half an inch. That is what a border is for.

## CHAPTER 06 — THE THIRD PARTY

*Act I · BRITISH EMBASSY, PETROGRAD · MARCH 1916*

**INT. BRITISH EMBASSY, PETROGRAD — MARCH 1916**

Frost on the windows. SERGEY SAZONOV (54) keeps his coat on. SIR GEORGE BUCHANAN (63) pours tea neither of them drinks. Sykes is a visitor here, and he knows it.

**SAZONOV**

Constantinople. The Bosphorus. The Dardanelles. We were promised.

**BUCHANAN**

And you will have them, if the war is won.

**SAZONOV**

Wars are won by signatures, Ambassador. Not by guns. Send me the map.

Sykes slides a folder across. Sazonov does not open it.

**SAZONOV (CONT'D)**

My government will agree to whatever you have already agreed between yourselves. We are not in the room, so we will sign the room.

**SYKES**

Your Excellency, there is no room.

**SAZONOV**

(a small, cold smile) There is always a room. In Petersburg we have a whole palace of them and nobody ever leaves.

He opens the folder at last. Studies the blue and red.

**SAZONOV (CONT'D)**

Palestine. You have made it brown.

**SYKES**

We have made it nobody's.

**SAZONOV**

That is how you make a war in fifty years.

He signs the covering note and hands it back without looking at Sykes.

## CHAPTER 07 — THE DESERT DESK

*Act I · ARAB BUREAU, CAIRO · 1916*

**INT. ARAB BUREAU, CAIRO — 1916**

GERTRUDE BELL (47) has three maps open and no patience. T.E. LAWRENCE (28) sits on the desk, boots on the chair. SALIM HADDAD (50), a Damascus merchant with ink on his fingers, waits to be asked.

**BELL**

The Arabs have given us their terms. One Arab state from Aleppo to Yemen.

**LAWRENCE**

And we have given them ours. Two different documents, both in English, both unsigned.

**BELL**

The Sharif's son is not a fool. He reads the newspapers.

**LAWRENCE**

He reads maps. That is worse.

Bell turns to Salim.

**BELL (CONT'D)**

You know the country. Read this line to me.

Salim studies the tracing paper. His finger stops.

**SALIM**

Here is my village. You have spelled it three ways. None of them is the name.

**BELL**

It is a working map, not a poem.

**SALIM**

It is a map of my country, madam. It should be both.

Lawrence smiles, then stops smiling because Salim is not joking.

**SALIM (CONT'D)**

When you draw this, ask yourself who will be standing on the line.

**LAWRENCE**

That is not our department.

**SALIM**

No. It never is.

## CHAPTER 08 — THE CHEMIST

*Act I · ROOM 309, LONDON · FEBRUARY 1916*

**INT. ROOM 309, LONDON — FEBRUARY 1916**

Rain on the window. CHAIM WEIZMANN (41) has a small bottle of acetone on the table between them, like a chess piece. Sykes cannot stop looking at it.

**WEIZMANN**

You want acetone. I want Palestine.

**SYKES**

That is a very large price for a solvent.

**WEIZMANN**

You are buying a war. I am selling you the thing that wins it.

Sykes picks up the bottle.

**SYKES**

Tell me about the Jewish state.

**WEIZMANN**

Not a state. A home. Somewhere a Jew is not a guest.

Sykes sets the bottle down. For a moment he is not a negotiator; he is a man with a bad conscience and a good imagination.

**SYKES**

I have spent my life drawing rooms for other people. Perhaps I can draw one for you.

**WEIZMANN**

And the Arabs?

**SYKES**

They will have their own.

**WEIZMANN**

In the same house?

**SYKES**

There are many rooms.

**WEIZMANN**

(quietly) There is one door.

## CHAPTER 09 — THE MAP ROOM

*Act I · FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON · MARCH 1916*

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, MAP ROOM — MARCH 1916**

Three in the morning. Coffee gone cold, cigarettes in the ashtray. The map has been redrawn so many times the paper is going through.

**SYKES**

If you take Mosul, you take the oil, the Kurds, and the trouble.

**PICOT**

If you take Mosul, you take the road to India and the trouble.

**SYKES**

Then we agree about the trouble.

**PICOT**

We agree about nothing. We are simply tired at the same hour.

Sykes laughs, unexpectedly, and Picot almost joins him. Almost.

**SYKES**

Haifa and Acre to us. Palestine international. Mosul to you, God help you.

**PICOT**

God has nothing to do with it. It is a straight line.

Sykes looks down at the table. Under the states map, another sheet is showing: tribes, roads, wells, the faint grey handwriting of people who live there.

**SYKES**

The day we sign this, someone will have to explain it to the people it is about.

**PICOT**

That is not our department.

He folds the map. The tribes disappear first.

## CHAPTER 10 — THE INITIALS

*Act I · FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON · 3 JANUARY 1916*

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON — 3 JANUARY 1916**

Two pens. One map. A clerk with a candle because the electric light has failed again.

**PICOT**

You are sentimental about it.

**SYKES**

I am sentimental about everything. It is cheaper than policy.

Picot initials first, small and exact. Sykes signs his name in full, then adds the date, then adds the year again because he is nervous.

**PICOT**

It will not hold.

**SYKES**

It will hold until it doesn't. That is what governments are.

**PICOT**

We have not made peace. We have made a delay.

**SYKES**

A delay is all a map ever is.

The clerk folds the map into an envelope. Sykes watches it go.

**INT. SYKES' HOUSE, LONDON — LATER**

Sykes writes at a small desk. His wife EDITH (34) is asleep in the next room.

My dear, I have made a line today that will outlive me. I hope it will not outlive the men who have to live on it.

He puts down the pen. In the mirror, a man who has just discovered that he is history.

## CHAPTER 11 — THE SIGNATURE

*Act II · FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON · 16 MAY 1916*

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON — 16 MAY 1916**

A small room, not a great one. No flags. SIR EDWARD GREY (54) and PAUL CAMBON (75) sit opposite each other with a single sheet between them.

**GREY**

This is not a treaty. It is a memorandum of understanding.

**CAMBON**

It is a treaty with a modest vocabulary.

**GREY**

It will be ratified by no parliament.

**CAMBON**

Then no parliament can betray it. How restful.

They exchange notes. The paper makes almost no sound. In the doorway, Sykes and Picot stand like witnesses at a wedding neither of them believes in.

**PICOT**

(under his breath) One thousand nine hundred and sixteen. Not a good year for maps.

**SYKES**

Every year is a good year for maps. That is the trouble.

Grey signs. Cambon signs. The clerk blots the ink with such care it looks like a blessing.

**GREY (CONT'D)**

This document does not exist.

**CAMBON**

Of course. I shall keep it in the safe with the other things that do not exist.

Outside, the guns of the Somme are six weeks away and nobody in the room can hear them yet.

## CHAPTER 12 — THE SILENCE

*Act II · HOUSE OF COMMONS, LONDON · JULY 1916*

**INT. HOUSE OF COMMONS — JULY 1916**

Heat, benches, papers. An MP (55) rises. Sykes watches from the gallery.

**MP**

Will the Right Honourable Gentleman confirm that conversations are taking place with our Allies regarding the future of Asia Minor?

**GREY**

I cannot make any statement on that subject.

**MP**

Can he deny that an agreement has been reached?

**GREY**

I cannot make any statement.

The House moves on to coal. In the corridor, a JOURNALIST (30) catches Sykes by the sleeve.

**JOURNALIST**

There is no agreement, then?

**SYKES**

There is no agreement.

**JOURNALIST**

Then why is there a map?

**SYKES**

Maps are cheap.

**JOURNALIST**

Men are not.

Sykes walks away. The journalist writes nothing, because there is nothing he can write that a censor will pass.

## CHAPTER 13 — THE REVOLT

*Act II · MECCA · JUNE 1916*

**EXT. MECCA — JUNE 1916**

Dawn. SHARIF HUSSEIN (62) fires a rifle from a balcony, once, into the sky. The shot is not aimed at anything. It is aimed at everyone.

Below, in the courtyard, FAISAL (31) watches the flag go up. An Arab flag, stitched in the night, already fraying at one corner.

**FAISAL**

It is done.

**HUSSEIN**

It is begun. Done is a word for Europeans.

T.E. LAWRENCE stands at the edge of the courtyard in dusty uniform, taking notes, and then not taking notes.

**FAISAL (CONT'D)**

Colonel. You promised us a country.

**LAWRENCE**

Your father was promised. There is a difference.

**FAISAL**

(smiling) Then I will be my father until the country arrives.

The crowd begins to move. Somewhere a drum, then gunfire that is celebration, then gunfire that is not.

**LAWRENCE**

(to himself) Which promise?

He shuts the notebook. The flag keeps snapping in the wind.

## CHAPTER 14 — THE ROAD TO AQABA

*Act II · THE NEFUD DESERT · 1917*

**EXT. THE NEFUD DESERT — 1917**

Heat like a wall. LAWRENCE (29), NASIR (26), and AUDA ABU TAYI (50) ride at the head of a column of camel riders. The desert is not empty; it is full of distance.

**AUDA**

The Turks expect us at the coast. They have guns at the coast.

**LAWRENCE**

Then we will come from the desert, where they have nothing.

**AUDA**

They have nothing because there is nothing. No water for sixty miles.

**LAWRENCE**

Then we will be there in one day and they will be dead in two.

Audа laughs, delighted, the way a man laughs at a good insult.

**AUDA**

You are a strange Englishman. You love the desert more than the English do.

**LAWRENCE**

The desert doesn't ask me to explain my government.

They ride. The camera holds on the line of camels and the enormous, indifferent sky.

**EXT. AQABA — DAWN**

Aqaba below them, white and blue and still. The sea at last.

**NASIR**

We came across the desert to take a port from the land side.

**LAWRENCE**

Yes.

**NASIR**

Nobody will believe it.

**LAWRENCE**

Good. Let them not believe it until it is ours.

## CHAPTER 15 — THE DECLARATION

*Act II · FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON · 2 NOVEMBER 1917*

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON — 2 NOVEMBER 1917**

A short letter on a long table. ARTHUR BALFOUR (69) reads it twice. CHAIM WEIZMANN (43) stands with his hat in his hands. Lord Rothschild waits by the window.

**BALFOUR**

"A national home for the Jewish people." Not a state.

**WEIZMANN**

Not yet.

**BALFOUR**

"Nothing shall be done which may prejudice the civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities."

**WEIZMANN**

We will honour that.

**BALFOUR**

It is in the letter, so someone will have to.

He signs. Sykes watches from behind Weizmann, and for once his face is not clever.

**SYKES**

(quietly, to Weizmann) You will have your home.

**WEIZMANN**

Thank you.

**SYKES**

God help the neighbours.

Weizmann turns, but Sykes is already looking at the window, at the grey November street, as if he can see who is standing on the other side of the letter.

## CHAPTER 16 — THE LEAK

*Act II · PETROGRAD · NOVEMBER 1917*

**INT. SMOLNY INSTITUTE, PETROGRAD — NOVEMBER 1917**

Boots, cigarette smoke, a press room lit by a single hanging bulb. G. SOKOLNIKOV (32) holds up a sheaf of papers like a trophy.

**SOKOLNIKOV**

Secret diplomacy. The treaties of the imperialists. We will publish them all.

A PRINTER reads over his shoulder, incredulous.

**PRINTER**

This one divides the Ottoman Empire.

**SOKOLNIKOV**

Then everyone will see what the war was for.

**PRINTER**

And the Arabs?

**SOKOLNIKOV**

The Arabs can read. That is the danger of teaching them.

The presses start. In London, a clerk at the Foreign Office reads the telegram and goes very still.

**INT. FOREIGN OFFICE, LONDON — CONTINUOUS**

SYKES reads the Russian text in translation. He puts it down carefully, as if it might go off.

**SYKES**

Now everyone will read what we never told them.

**AIDE**

Deny it, sir?

**SYKES**

No. It's in their hands. The only thing left is to be quick about what we meant.

He looks at the map on the wall. It looks back.

## CHAPTER 17 — THE QUESTION

*Act II · CAIRO · DECEMBER 1917*

**INT. ARAB BUREAU, CAIRO — DECEMBER 1917**

Faisal sits with the published text in Arabic. Lawrence stands. Neither of them has slept.

**FAISAL**

This says my country is to be divided.

**LAWRENCE**

It says the districts west of Damascus, Homs, Hama and Aleppo are not included in the Arab state.

**FAISAL**

I can read, Colonel.

**LAWRENCE**

Then you can read that we promised your father the opposite.

Silence. Faisal folds the paper once, very precisely.

**FAISAL**

My father is an old man in Mecca who believes Englishmen. I am not old, and I no longer believe anyone.

**LAWRENCE**

What do you want me to do?

**FAISAL**

Tell me which promise is the real one. Then I will know which war I am fighting.

Lawrence has no answer. He looks at the map on the wall and sees, for the first time, that it is not a map of a country. It is a map of a conversation between two governments.

**LAWRENCE**

Both of them are real. That is the problem with promises.

**FAISAL**

No. The problem is the men who make them twice.

## CHAPTER 18 — THE PROMISE

*Act II · DERAA · 1918*

**EXT. DERAA — 1918**

Dust, flies, a broken railway. SYKES, in uniform and far from London, waits beside a staff car. FAISAL rides up with a small escort.

**FAISAL**

You are the man who drew the line.

**SYKES**

I am one of them.

**FAISAL**

You drew my country on a napkin and then went to lunch.

**SYKES**

It was a very long lunch.

Faisal does not smile. Sykes's wit dies in the heat.

**SYKES (CONT'D)**

When the war is over, the promises will be sorted out. I will do what I can.

**FAISAL**

You have already done it. That is the difficulty with promises. They are not made when they are spoken. They are made when someone draws.

**SYKES**

Then let me be useful now.

**FAISAL**

You can tell your government that a country is not a line on paper. It is a hundred thousand people who know the name of the well.

Sykes takes out a notebook. For a moment he is a man trying to write down the thing he has spent his life drawing over.

**FAISAL (CONT'D)**

Write carefully. It will be quoted.

## CHAPTER 19 — THE LAST CARTOGRAPHER

*Act II · HÔTEL DE CRILLON, PARIS · FEBRUARY 1919*

**INT. HÔTEL DE CRILLON, PARIS — FEBRUARY 1919**

Outside, the Peace Conference. Inside, a hotel corridor turned into a hospital. Sykes lies under a sheet, breathing badly. Influenza has taken the colour out of him.

PICOT stands at the foot of the bed with his hat in his hand, which he has never done before.

**SYKES**

You came.

**PICOT**

I was in the building. There is a great deal of building.

**SYKES**

Did they give you Palestine?

**PICOT**

They gave me a committee.

**SYKES**

(almost a laugh) Then we both lost the same way.

Picot sits. The two men look at each other across four years of maps.

**PICOT**

You were the only Englishman who could be moved by a map.

**SYKES**

It is a low bar.

**PICOT**

It is the only one that matters.

Sykes closes his eyes. Picot waits. Then, to the aide outside the door:

**PICOT (CONT'D)**

Who draws now?

**AIDE**

I don't know, sir.

**PICOT**

No. Neither do I.

## CHAPTER 20 — THE PEACE

*Act II · QUAI D'ORSAY, PARIS · 1919*

**INT. QUAI D'ORSAY, PARIS — 1919**

The Peace Conference. Chandeliers, interpreters, a map of the world on an easel like a painting for sale.

FAISAL, in robes, speaks. LAWRENCE sits behind him in a British uniform, which is its own kind of statement.

**FAISAL**

The Arabs ask for one thing: that the promises made to them in their hour of need be kept in their hour of victory.

**CLEMENCEAU**

Which promises? There appear to have been several.

**FAISAL**

That is not my fault. I only made one.

Lloyd George studies his papers. Clemenceau studies Lloyd George. Nobody studies Faisal.

**LLOYD GEORGE**

We are all friends here. The question is administration.

**FAISAL**

It is not administration. It is sovereignty.

**CLEMENCEAU**

(gently) Sovereignty is administration with better furniture.

Faisal looks at Lawrence. Lawrence looks at the table.

**FAISAL (CONT'D)**

Then I will take the furniture. And I will remember who sold it.

## CHAPTER 21 — THE COMMISSION

*Act III · DAMASCUS · 1919*

**INT. A COURTYARD HOUSE, DAMASCUS — 1919**

An American flag on a chair, because there is nowhere better to put it. CHARLES CRANE (61) and HENRY KING (32) sit with notebooks. SALIM HADDAD (53) sits opposite with his cap in his hands.

**CRANE**

We have come to ask what the people of Syria want. Not what the governments want.

**SALIM**

Then you will be the first.

**KING**

Do you want an American mandate?

**SALIM**

We want a country. If a mandate is the price, we will pay it, but do not call the price a gift.

Crane writes this down slowly, as if the slowness will make it more true.

**SALIM (CONT'D)**

There is a map. The English and the French have already drawn it.

**CRANE**

We have seen the map.

**SALIM**

Then you know it was drawn by men who never asked us where the wells are.

**KING**

What would you have us say to Paris?

**SALIM**

Tell them a country is not a line. Tell them we have been here since before their language had a word for here.

Crane closes the notebook.

**CRANE**

They will not listen.

**SALIM**

No. But you will have written it down.

## CHAPTER 22 — THE CONGRESS

*Act III · DAMASCUS · MARCH 1920*

**EXT. DAMASCUS — MARCH 1920**

Bunting across the street. A crowd of thousands. On a balcony, the Syrian Congress with FAISAL at its centre. SALIM and his daughter NADIA (19) watch from below.

**NADIA**

They are declaring him king.

**SALIM**

They are declaring a country. The king is the easy part.

Above, a delegate reads the resolution. The words carry badly in the wind; only fragments reach the street.

**DELEGATE (V.O.)**

...complete independence... a constitutional monarchy... the Arab lands, indivisible...

The crowd roars. Nadia does not. She is watching her father, who is watching the balcony.

**NADIA**

You are not cheering.

**SALIM**

I am counting. There are three governments in Paris who will read this tomorrow and one of them will already have a map.

**NADIA**

Then why did you come?

**SALIM**

Because when they take it, I want to have been here.

He takes her hand. The flag goes up, and the wind tears at it exactly the way it did in Mecca.

## CHAPTER 23 — SAN REMO

*Act III · VILLA DEVACHAN, SAN REMO · APRIL 1920*

**INT. VILLA DEVACHAN, SAN REMO — APRIL 1920**

Palms outside, a string quartet somewhere, and the Supreme Council deciding the fate of countries between courses.

LLOYD GEORGE (57), MILLERAND (61), BERTHELOT (58). A map on the wall. No Arabs.

**BERTHELOT**

The French mandate over Syria and Lebanon. As agreed.

**LLOYD GEORGE**

And Mesopotamia and Palestine as British mandates.

**MILLERAND**

Palestine. With the Balfour Declaration attached.

**LLOYD GEORGE**

Attached, yes.

**BERTHELOT**

It is a strange thing to attach a promise to a mandate.

**LLOYD GEORGE**

It is a strange thing to govern. We do the best we can.

A secretary leans in and whispers to Lloyd George.

**LLOYD GEORGE (CONT'D)**

Tell them we will receive a delegation after the session.

**SECRETARY**

There is no delegation, sir.

**LLOYD GEORGE**

Then tell them we will receive one after the session.

The quartet plays on. On the wall, the map of the Ottoman Empire is now a map of possessions.

## CHAPTER 24 — MAYSALUN

*Act III · MAYSALUN PASS · 24 JULY 1920*

**EXT. MAYSALUN PASS — 24 JULY 1920**

Dust, heat, a handful of soldiers and volunteers against a French column with artillery. YUSUF AL-AZMA (36) walks the line, a sword at his belt, a cigarette he does not smoke.

**AL-AZMA**

When the guns start, do not run. If we must die, we die here, where the road enters the country.

SALIM is with the volunteers, too old for this, carrying ammunition.

**SALIM**

My daughter thinks I am in the souk.

**AL-AZMA**

Then she is a sensible woman.

The first shell lands short. The second takes the ridge.

**EXT. THE ROAD — LATER**

Smoke. A French armoured car passes. Salim lies at the edge of the road, his coat over his face, his notebook of village names fallen open in the dust.

A French SOLDIER picks it up, looks at the Arabic, and drops it again.

**INT. DAMASCUS — NIGHT**

Nadia opens the door to a man in uniform who cannot meet her eyes. She does not scream. She takes the notebook and holds it against her chest.

**NADIA**

He said they would take it.

**OFFICER**

Madam—

**NADIA**

He said they would take it, and he went anyway.

## CHAPTER 25 — THE CAIRO CONFERENCE

*Act III · SEMIRAMIS HOTEL, CAIRO · MARCH 1921*

**INT. SEMIRAMIS HOTEL, CAIRO — MARCH 1921**

Winston Churchill (46) in a panama hat, GERTRUDE BELL (52) with a folder, T.E. LAWRENCE (32) looking like a man who has been awake since 1917.

**CHURCHILL**

We need a king for Iraq. Preferably one who is already famous and not too attached to Damascus.

**BELL**

Faisal.

**CHURCHILL**

He has just been thrown out of Syria by the French. Won't that look like a demotion?

**BELL**

It will look like a promotion if we print it on the right paper.

**LAWRENCE**

And Abdullah?

**CHURCHILL**

Transjordan. It is small, it is quiet, and it is not Palestine. That should keep everyone unhappy.

**BELL**

We are drawing a country on a napkin again.

**CHURCHILL**

This time it is a hotel napkin. That is an improvement.

Bell opens the folder. A map of Mesopotamia with three provinces pencilled together.

**BELL (CONT'D)**

Mosul, Baghdad, Basra. Three places that have nothing in common except a river and us.

**CHURCHILL**

Then give them a king with no army and a promise of oil. It is how England was built.

## CHAPTER 26 — THE BORDERS

*Act III · LAUSANNE · 1923*

**INT. LAUSANNE, CONFERENCE ROOM — 1923**

Yet another room, yet another map. ISMET PASHA (39) smokes continuously. LORD CURZON (64) has the look of a man who has been in this room since 1919.

**CURZON**

Mosul is part of Iraq.

**ISMET**

Mosul is part of Turkey. It has been for four hundred years.

**CURZON**

It has been part of the Ottoman Empire. That empire no longer exists.

**ISMET**

Then neither does your map. And yet here we are, drawing it.

Curzon's pencil hovers over the mountains.

**CURZON**

The League will decide.

**ISMET**

The League will decide in your favour, because you have the oil and the navy. Let us save a year and say so.

**EXT. BABA GURGUR — 1927**

A gusher. Black oil into the sky, men running, laughing, ruined. A British ENGINEER wipes his face and grins.

**ENGINEER**

There she is. The border just became valuable.

**INT. MAP ROOM, LONDON — LATER**

An official draws the Iraq–Turkey border along the mountains, then stops at the oil field and carefully goes around it.

**OFFICIAL**

(to himself) The line is not geography. It is a contract.

## CHAPTER 27 — TWO KINGS

*Act III · BAGHDAD AND AMMAN · 1920s*

**INT. BAGHDAD PALACE — 1924**

Faisal (39), heavier now, in a dark suit, receives a British adviser. On the wall, a portrait of his father and a map of Iraq.

**ADVISER**

The treaty requires British officers to remain.

**FAISAL**

The treaty requires a great deal. It does not require me to pretend I like it.

**ADVISER**

You are a king now. Kings pretend.

**FAISAL**

I was a king in Damascus for four months. I have learned what a mandate is. It is a crown with a string on it.

He turns to the map.

**FAISAL (CONT'D)**

My brother in Amman has the other half of the same promise.

**INT. AMMAN, SMALL PALACE — 1925**

ABDULLAH (43) pours tea for a Bedouin sheikh. Through the window, the desert.

**ABDULLAH**

They gave me a country with no resources and a border with everybody.

**SHEIKH**

Then you are the only man in the region who cannot be bought.

**ABDULLAH**

(laughing) I can be bought. I am simply expensive.

The laugh fades. He looks at the desert the way his brother looked at the map.

## CHAPTER 28 — THE PIPE

*Act III · KIRKUK–HAIFA PIPELINE · 1934*

**EXT. THE SYRIAN DESERT — 1934**

A pipeline runs to the horizon, two pipes side by side, a road beside them. A young ENGINEER walks the line with a local guide, HAMZI (40).

**HAMZI**

This pipe crosses my grandfather's land.

**ENGINEER**

It crosses three countries.

**HAMZI**

It crosses one country and two borders. That is not the same thing.

The engineer has no answer, so he checks the pressure gauge.

**ENGINEER**

At Haifa it goes into a ship and becomes money.

**HAMZI**

At Kirkuk it comes out of the ground and becomes money. Here it is just a pipe.

**ENGINEER**

That is the most political sentence I have heard all year.

**HAMZI**

I am a guide. I am paid to notice things.

They walk on. Behind them the pipe shines in the sun, a straight line with a pulse.

## CHAPTER 29 — THE STRAIGHT LINE

*Act III · THE SYRIAN DESERT · 1938*

**EXT. THE SYRIAN DESERT — 1938**

A surveying team with a theodolite, a tent, and a fleet of cars. A BRITISH SURVEYOR (45) sights along the instrument while a French officer checks a map.

**FRENCH OFFICER**

Straight to the tell.

**SURVEYOR**

It is straight. It is simply not where anyone lives.

An old BEDOUIN WOMAN (70) watches from the flap of a goat-hair tent. A BOY translates for her.

**BOY**

She says the wells are on the other side.

**SURVEYOR**

Then she can cross at the post.

**BOY**

She says her family is on both sides.

**SURVEYOR**

Then she can choose a side.

The boy translates. The old woman says something short.

**BOY**

She says her family did not choose to be a border.

The surveyor takes off his hat and wipes his forehead. He does not answer. He goes back to the instrument.

**SURVEYOR**

Mark it. The line goes through.

## CHAPTER 30 — THE COUP

Iraq for the Iraqis. No more treaties, no more advisers, no more lines drawn in London.

**RASHID ALI (CONT'D)**

The British have landed at Basra.

All of them.

## CHAPTER 31 — THE PARTITION

*Act III · JERUSALEM AND NEW YORK · 1947*

**INT. UNSCOP HEARING, JERUSALEM — 1947**

A committee table. Maps with hatched areas. NADIA HADDAD (46), a teacher in Haifa, holds her father's notebook.

**NADIA**

You are drawing a line through a city where my neighbours and I buy the same bread.

**CHAIR**

We are trying to find a solution.

**NADIA**

You are trying to find a majority. They are not the same.

**CHAIR**

The mandate is ending. Someone must govern.

**NADIA**

Someone has governed us for thirty years and we did not ask for the first line. Now you offer us a second one and call it self-determination.

She opens the notebook.

**NADIA (CONT'D)**

My father wrote down every village between here and the desert. He said, when they take the country, we will have been here. I am here. Where will the line be?

Nobody answers her.

**INT. UNITED NATIONS, NEW YORK — 29 NOVEMBER 1947**

A gavel. Applause. The map of Palestine divided into three, in blue and orange, like a stained-glass window over a door that is closing.

## CHAPTER 32 — THE NAKBA

*Act III · HAIFA · APRIL 1948*

**EXT. HAIFA — APRIL 1948**

Smoke over the port. A column of people on the road, carrying everything that fits. NADIA (47) walks with a suitcase and her father's notebook. Her daughter MARIAM (9) holds her hand.

**MARIAM**

Are we coming back?

**NADIA**

Yes.

**MARIAM**

When?

**NADIA**

When the line moves.

A British soldier waves the column through. Behind them, a house with a blue door and a lemon tree in the yard.

**MARIAM**

You said we would never leave.

**NADIA**

I said we would never be far. Look.

She points at the ridge. On it, a white building, and beyond it, hills.

**NADIA (CONT'D)**

We are one hour from home. We will always be one hour from home.

The suitcase handle breaks. She picks up the clothes and keeps walking. She does not look back, because looking back is how you become a refugee twice.

**INT. A ROOM IN LEBANON — NIGHT**

Nadia opens the notebook. The page with the village names. She adds one more, in pencil: Haifa.

**NURI**

An alliance for the region. Stability.

(to the officer beside him) An alliance with the people who drew the region.

GAMAL ABDEL NASSER (38) speaks into a microphone, and the whole Arab world leans in.

We drew it perfectly. That's the problem.

## CHAPTER 34 — THE BA'ATH

*Act IV · DAMASCUS AND BAGHDAD · 1963–1968*

**INT. DAMASCUS, PARTY OFFICE — 1963**

Young men in suits, banners, a portrait of the nation as a single idea. A SPEAKER (35) at a lectern.

**SPEAKER**

One Arab nation, with an immortal mission. The borders of 1916 are the borders of our humiliation.

Applause. In the corner, an old woman — NADIA (62) — sits with a notebook she did not bring to be read.

**NADIA**

(to the young man beside her) He says the borders are a humiliation.

**YOUNG MAN**

They are.

**NADIA**

Then why does he keep standing inside one?

**INT. BAGHDAD, RADIO STATION — 1968**

A different party, the same speech. A portrait replaces a portrait. Outside, a tank turns in the square.

**ANNOUNCER**

The revolution has triumphed.

**INT. NADIA'S ROOM, DAMASCUS — NIGHT**

Nadia listens to the radio and closes her father's notebook.

**MARIAM (30)**

You should sleep.

**NADIA**

Every generation says it will erase the line. Then it draws a new one and calls it a revolution.

**MARIAM**

And us?

**NADIA**

We keep the names. Somebody has to be the memory.

## CHAPTER 35 — THE LONG WAR

*Act IV · THE IRAQ–IRAN BORDER · 1980–1988*

**EXT. THE IRAQ–IRAN BORDER — 1982**

A trench through a date-palm grove. Young Iraqi soldiers, some no older than sixteen, wait. An OFFICER (29) looks at a map that shows a line no one can see from here.

**SOLDIER**

Sir, where does Iraq end?

**OFFICER**

At the water.

**SOLDIER**

Which water?

**OFFICER**

(pointing) That ditch. Then the marsh. Then the river.

**SOLDIER**

My village is on the other side.

**OFFICER**

Then your village is on the other side.

Artillery. The men duck. The officer folds the map and puts it inside his coat, over his heart, as if it might stop something.

**INT. BAGHDAD, HOSPITAL — 1988**

Rows of beds. A nurse moves between them. On a wall, a poster of the president.

**NURSE**

(to a boy) What is your name?

**BOY**

I don't remember.

**NURSE**

Try.

**BOY**

I remember the map. It was green and brown.

It was carved out of Basra by the British in 1922. A line around an oil field. They invented it to keep us weak.

The world recognises the border, Excellency.

Then the world can defend it.

## CHAPTER 37 — THE SANCTIONS

*Act IV · BAGHDAD · 1997*

**INT. BAGHDAD, A SMALL APARTMENT — 1997**

A bare room. A woman, HUDA (34), divides a bag of rice into seven small portions. Her daughter LINA (13) watches.

**LINA**

Is it because of the war?

**HUDA**

It is because of the peace.

**LINA**

I don't understand.

**HUDA**

Nobody understands. That is what a sanction is. A punishment for people who did not do the thing.

Lina picks up a schoolbook. On the cover, a map of Iraq.

**LINA**

They made us learn this map. Every year. The same map.

**HUDA**

Then learn it well. One day you will have to explain it to someone who has never been here.

Lina traces the border with her finger, the way Sykes did with a pencil, the way her great-grandfather did with a notebook.

The rest is a country.

Then I will do it back.

You are looking at the original sin of the modern Middle East.

I am looking at a piece of paper with a line on it.

That is what an original sin is.

**LINA**

**HUDA**

They are drawing a new map. Everyone has a map now.

What does it look like?

The same. Only the ink is wet.

## CHAPTER 40 — THE CALIPHATE

*Act IV · MOSUL · JUNE 2014*

**EXT. MOSUL, THE SYRIA–IRAQ BORDER — JUNE 2014**

A bulldozer. A crowd. Men in black beat the sand and drive a blade through the border berm. Cameras film it from three angles.

**MILITANT**

(to camera) We have broken the border of Sykes-Picot.

In a Berlin apartment, LINA (30) watches the video on a laptop. Her mother's voice on the phone.

**HUDA (V.O.)**

Are you watching?

**LINA**

Yes.

**HUDA (V.O.)**

They say they have ended it.

**LINA**

They have not ended it. They are its children. They are drawing the same line and calling it a caliphate.

**HUDA (V.O.)**

How can you be sure?

**LINA**

Because they are standing on it. You cannot destroy a border by standing on it and filming yourself.

**LINA (CONT'D)**

They need the line. Without it they are just men in a desert.

## CHAPTER 41 — THE REFUGEE

*Act IV · THE AEGEAN SEA · 2015*

**EXT. THE AEGEAN SEA — NIGHT**

A rubber boat, overfull, the engine coughing. A smuggler cuts the rope and turns back. Twenty people, a child, an old woman, a man who cannot swim.

LINA (31) is not in the boat. She watches from a rescue ship, holding a clipboard, translating.

**COASTGUARD**

How many?

**LINA**

Twenty-two. No. Twenty-three. There is a child under the bench.

They pull people aboard. A SYRIAN BOY (7) will not let go of a plastic bag.

**LINA (CONT'D)**

What is in the bag?

**BOY**

My map. For school.

She opens it. A school atlas of Syria, water-stained, the borders still printed in blue.

**LINA**

You brought your map.

**BOY**

It was the only thing that fits in the bag.

Lina looks at the sea, and then at the boy, and does not tell him that the map is wrong, or that it is right, or that it is the reason he is here.

## CHAPTER 42 — THE LINE

*Act IV · BERLIN · PRESENT DAY*

**INT. BERLIN, ARCHIVE — PRESENT DAY**

A reading room. White gloves. LINA (40) sits opposite an ARCHIVIST (55), who has laid a folded map on the table.

**ARCHIVIST**

Sykes–Picot. 1916. The copy the Foreign Office kept.

**LINA**

May I?

**ARCHIVIST**

Gloves.

She opens it. Blue, red, brown. A line from Acre to Kirkuk. Her great-grandfather's village sits exactly on it.

**LINA (CONT'D)**

Here. This is the name they spelled three ways.

**ARCHIVIST**

You know the map.

**LINA**

I have been looking at it my whole life.

**ARCHIVIST**

It is only paper.

**LINA**

It is never only paper.

She takes out her great-grandmother's notebook and lays it beside the map. The names in Arabic, in pencil, in a dead man's hand.

**LINA (CONT'D)**

Two men drew a line in a room in London. A hundred years later, I am still explaining it to people who were not in the room.

**ARCHIVIST**

Would you like a copy?

**LINA**

No.

She closes the map, carefully, the way you close a door in a house you are leaving.

**LINA (CONT'D)**

I would like to catalogue the names.

**EXT. BERLIN — DUSK**

Lina walks out into the street. Trams, rain, a hundred languages. She carries the notebook, not the map.

*THE LINE REMAINS.*

*THE END.*
