# CHAPTER 13 — THE RESERVOIR

**EXT. THE OLD RESERVOIR — 3:48 A.M.**

A hill above the city where the waterworks gave up in 1974. Chain-link, then nothing. The tank is a drained concrete bowl the size of a cathedral, open to the sky, with the city laid out under it like something dropped and left.

Rain. Not heavy. The kind that gets into your collar and stays.

IRIS comes up the service road on foot, a flight case in one hand, a brass fragment on a cord around her neck. She stops at the lip and looks down.

Twelve emitters on tripods. A generator. A folding chair, centre, on the dry floor of the tank.

And a man at the rail, sleeves rolled, hands behind his back, watching the city the way other men watch the sea.

VANE
You came alone.

IRIS
You asked.

VANE
I asked because I knew it would work.

She comes down the ladder. He does not offer to help, and she does not need it.

IRIS
Where is he?

VANE
Tomas is having a cup of tea. He's had a difficult six weeks and he's been very good about it.

IRIS
Show me him and I'll answer your question.

VANE
You haven't asked a question.

IRIS
You want me to. Get on with it.

He almost smiles. He is thinner than she remembers, and calmer in a way that is worse than anger. The last time she saw him he was shouting at a grant committee.

**INT. RESERVOIR — SERVICE ROOM — CONTINUOUS**

A concrete office with a kettle, two chairs and a strip light. TOMAS (34) sits with a mug and a paperback he is not reading.

He stands when they come in. Polite. A man meeting his wife's colleague.

IRIS
Tomas. I'm Iris.

TOMAS
I know who you are. You wrote the paper.

IRIS
I wrote *a* paper.

TOMAS
He says you're the only one who understands it.

IRIS
He says a lot of things in the third person about a woman standing in front of him.

Tomas laughs. It is a real laugh, and it stops her.

IRIS (CONT'D)
How long have you been coming here?

TOMAS
Six weeks. Thursdays.

IRIS
What do you do on Thursdays?

TOMAS
(considering it)
I sit in the chair.

IRIS
And?

TOMAS
And it gets quiet.

IRIS
Why did you start?

Tomas looks at Vane, then decides something.

TOMAS
My daughter died in March. I prayed for four days. I was very sincere. Then I washed my hands and went back to work, and I have not been able to pray since.

IRIS
That's grief, Tomas. That's not a defect.

TOMAS
I know that.

(beat)

It just stopped being true. All of it. Overnight, like a key turning. I came here because I thought if a machine could do that, then it was never true in the first place, and I wouldn't have to be angry with her for believing it.

Iris has no answer to that. She looks at Vane, who is standing with his hands in his pockets, entirely comfortable.

IRIS
You picked him.

VANE
He picked us.

IRIS
You waited for a man in the worst week of his life.

VANE
Yes.

IRIS
That's not a study.

VANE
No. It's a demonstration.

**INT. RESERVOIR — TANK FLOOR — 4:04 A.M.**

Twelve emitters in a ring. Tomas in the chair. Iris stands at the console with her phone already recording, the red light on, held out where he can see it. He sees it. He does not mind.

IRIS
What is the amplitude?

VANE
Fourteen.

IRIS
And sixteen is what?

VANE
Permanent.

IRIS
You're not going to sixteen.

VANE
Not tonight. Tonight is a courtesy. You should see the thing before you decide whether to spend your life stopping it.

IRIS
I decided in the car.

VANE
No, you decided in the corridor, and then you came down the ladder anyway. That's not a woman who's decided.

He turns the master switch.

The hum comes down the tank from the walls. It doesn't hurt. That's the thing she will remember: it doesn't hurt at all. It arrives in the bones and stays there, patient, like a hand on a shoulder.

Tomas's hands open. His face does not change; it *unfocuses*, the way a face does when a conversation ends and the other person has walked away.

IRIS
Tomas. What did you pray for?

TOMAS
(thinking about it, honestly)
I don't know. Something about a — there was a thing. It was important.

IRIS
What thing?

TOMAS
I can see it. I can't get to it.

Vane turns it off.

The silence afterwards has edges. Tomas blinks, looks at his hands, and starts to cry — not from grief, from the *absence* of it; the cupboard is bare and he can see the shelves.

TOMAS
Can I go home?

VANE
Yes.

TOMAS
Is it going to come back?

VANE
No.

Tomas nods. He puts on his coat like a man leaving a dentist. At the top of the ladder he stops and looks down at them.

TOMAS
(to Iris, politely)
I'm sorry about your sister.

Then he is gone, and the rain takes him.

**INT. RESERVOIR — TANK FLOOR — CONTINUOUS**

Iris hasn't moved. The phone is still recording.

IRIS
Say the sentence you said to him.

VANE
Which one?

IRIS
Before the switch. The one you say to all of them.

VANE
(mildly)
I say: you won't lose anything. You'll just stop needing it.

IRIS
And they believe you because you believe it.

VANE
I believe it because it's the most precisely true thing I have ever measured. Nothing is lost, Iris. The memory is intact. The doctrine is intact. What's gone is the *weight*. The part that makes a man get up at four in the morning and be frightened.

IRIS
That's not weight. That's the person.

VANE
That's the habit.

(beat)

You wrote the caveats. You wrote them as *practical*. "Do not exceed sixteen percent in a confined space." "Do not expose a subject twice within ninety days." You wrote them the way a woman writes a warning label on a bottle, because you could already see the bottle.

IRIS
I wrote them because I could see exactly this and I thought the world would be careful.

VANE
It wasn't.

IRIS
Neither are you.

He looks at her for a long moment, and there is something almost like affection in it.

VANE
No. But I'm the one who was right about that.

**EXT. THE OLD RESERVOIR — 4:40 A.M.**

She walks down the hill. Behind her, the generator coughs and dies, and the tank goes dark.

The phone is still recording. She stops under a streetlight and plays it back, and hears her own voice asking what a book is for, and Tomas saying he can see it but can't get to it.

She keeps walking.

# CHAPTER 14 — WARD NINE

**INT. ST. ALDWYN'S — RECEPTION — MORNING**

A municipal hospital doing its best. Christmas tinsel still up in February. A queue for the pharmacy.

Iris at the desk with a clipboard she does not have authority to hold.

IRIS
I'm from the sound lab. Environmental audit. Ward Nine.

RECEPTIONIST
Second floor, then left, then ask again. There's no sign.

IRIS
There's no sign.

RECEPTIONIST
(not looking up)
There's no sign.

**INT. ST. ALDWYN'S — WARD NINE — MORNING**

Twenty beds. Twenty people sitting up. A trolley of untouched breakfasts.

NURSE ADEYEMI (40s) is doing the observations. She does them briskly and well, the way you handle something you cannot fix.

IRIS
Who's the consultant?

ADEYEMI
There isn't one. It's a *recovery cohort*.

IRIS
That's not a medical term.

ADEYEMI
No. It's a form.

Iris walks the row. A man making a paper bird. A woman looking at a magazine without turning the page. Nobody is distressed. That's the worst of it — nobody is distressed.

IRIS
Show me one who was religious.

ADEYEMI
(pointing)
Bed four.

**INT. WARD NINE — BED FOUR — CONTINUOUS**

MRS. OKONKWO (60s), in a cardigan she chose herself, hands folded.

IRIS
Mrs. Okonkwo. I'm Iris.

MRS. OKONKWO
(pleasantly)
Good morning.

IRIS
Were you a lay reader?

MRS. OKONKWO
Thirty-one years.

IRIS
At St. Aidan's?

MRS. OKONKWO
I know that name.

IRIS
What's your favourite hymn?

MRS. OKONKWO
(without hesitation)
"There is a green hill far away."

IRIS
Sing it for me.

The woman opens her mouth. Nothing comes. Not shyness — her mouth is open, she is willing, she has the words in the front of her head where she has kept them for thirty-one years.

Nothing comes.

MRS. OKONKWO
(confused, for the first time)
It's there. It's just not — I don't know what it's *for*.

Iris sits down on the end of the bed, hard, as if her legs had gone.

IRIS
(quietly)
What's a hymn for?

MRS. OKONKWO
(genuinely considering)
Something to do after the sermon?

IRIS
And what's a book for?

MRS. OKONKWO
Reading.

IRIS
Why?

MRS. OKONKWO
(a long pause, then, honestly)
I don't know how to answer that.

**INT. WARD NINE — NURSES' STATION — CONTINUOUS**

ADEYEMI
They're not depressed. Depression you can talk to. These ones answer every question you ask and none of the answers mean anything to them.

IRIS
Who authorized this ward?

ADEYEMI
It's not a ward.

IRIS
Who authorized it?

ADEYEMI
There's a man comes on Thursdays. Grey coat. He signs a form and takes the folder.

IRIS
What's his name?

ADEYEMI
(nodding at Iris's coat)
He had a lanyard with the same logo as yours.

**INT. WARD NINE — CONTINUOUS**

Iris stands very still. Behind her, in bed twelve, a boy of six is drawing. A house, a sun, and a bird, over and over on the same page.

She doesn't notice him. She is already taking out her phone, and the ward's fluorescent lights are already buzzing at the edge of the recording in a way she will hear later and hate.
# CHAPTER 15 — KANE'S FIRST DOUBT

**INT. ST. ALDWYN'S — CORRIDOR — AFTERNOON**

KANE comes down the second-floor corridor with a visitor's badge and a clipboard that is doing all his talking for him. Nobody stops a man with a clipboard.

At the ward door he stops.

**INT. WARD NINE — CONTINUOUS**

Twenty people. Nineteen of them look up at him. Bed four does not.

He walks the row slowly, the way a man walks a neighbourhood he used to live in. At the foot of bed twelve he stops.

The BOY (6) is sitting cross-legged on the covers with a red crayon. He is drawing, with enormous concentration, a house, a sun, and a bird.

KANE
What's that?

BOY
It's my picture.

KANE
I can see that.
(crouches)
What's the bird called?

The boy looks at the bird. He looks at Kane. He looks back at the bird.

BOY
(patiently, as if explaining to a slow adult)
It's the bird in the picture.

KANE
No — what's its *name.*

BOY
(thinking about it)
It hasn't got one.

KANE
What's the house called?

BOY
House.

KANE
What's your name?

The boy's face does something complicated. He knows this one. He has been asked this one.

BOY
Danny.

KANE
Right. Danny. Do you like the bird?

The boy looks at the picture for a long time.

BOY
I draw it every day.

KANE
That's not what I asked.

BOY
(a small frown — the first real expression on the ward)
I don't know how to answer that.

**INT. WARD NINE — NURSES' STATION — CONTINUOUS**

Adeyemi watches him from the desk, arms folded, not interfering.

KANE
(coming over, quietly)
How long has he been like that?

ADEYEMI
Three weeks. He came in for a hearing test and the machine was on for six minutes.

KANE
Six minutes.

ADEYEMI
Sixteen percent, they said. I looked it up. I shouldn't have been able to look it up.

KANE
(writing nothing on the clipboard)
Is he in pain?

ADEYEMI
(a beat)
He's not in anything.

**INT. STAIRWELL — CONTINUOUS**

Kane stands with both hands on the railing, looking down four flights into the grey light of the lobby.

He takes out his phone. It rings three times.

VANE (V.O.)
Yes.

KANE
It's Kane. The kid in the ward.

VANE (V.O.)
Which kid?

KANE
Twelve. Six years old. He draws the same bird every day and he can't tell me if he likes it.

VANE (V.O.)
(a pause)
Danny Achebe. Six minutes, sixteen percent. I know.

KANE
Then why is he still in there?

VANE (V.O.)
Because the ward is the control, Marek. It's the number the whole programme stands on. If I move him out, there is no sixteen, there is no permanent, and there is no argument for closing the sanctuaries.

KANE
He's six.

VANE (V.O.)
Yes.

KANE
Say something else.

VANE (V.O.)
(not unkindly)
What would you like me to say? That I feel badly? I don't. I did the arithmetic and the arithmetic was correct, and the only thing that's changed since this morning is that you have seen the boy.

KANE
That's not an answer.

VANE (V.O.)
It's the answer I have. Go home, Marek. We'll speak Thursday.

He hangs up. Vane hangs up first.

Kane stands in the stairwell for a long moment. Then he puts the phone away, takes out the clipboard, and writes, carefully, on the blank page: *Danny Achebe. Ward 9. 6 yrs.*

He folds the page and puts it in his inside pocket.

**INT. HUB — OBSERVATION ROOM — SAME TIME**

Vane at the glass, hands behind his back, watching the orientation hall below. He is not looking at his phone.

SARA, at the desk behind him, has heard the whole call. She types nothing.

VANE
(without turning)
Note the deviation.

SARA
In the weekly?

VANE
In the log.

SARA
(quietly)
Yes.

She opens the log. Types: *M. Kane — expressed doubt re: control group.* Deletes it. Types again: *M. Kane — completed ward audit.* Saves.

# CHAPTER 16 — RETALIATION

**EXT. THE HUB — SERVICE ENTRANCE — 10:40 P.M.**

A loading bay, a wall of recycling bins, a door propped with a brick. BRENNAN (31), a technician, comes out for a cigarette and the last five minutes of his shift.

Two men in grey coats are standing by the bins. He knows them both. One takes the collection at the eight o'clock.

BRENNAN
(a beat, then, trying it)
Evening, Brother.

FIRST BROTHER
Brennan. Walk with us.

BRENNAN
I've got a cigarette.

FIRST BROTHER
Bring it.

**INT. HUB — STAIRWELL — CONTINUOUS**

Four flights up in the service stairwell, the kind with painted concrete and no windows.

They don't ask about the weapon. They don't ask about the array. They ask about the archivist.

SECOND BROTHER
You've been seen with the woman from the sound lab.

BRENNAN
I've been seen with a *lift key*.

FIRST BROTHER
Two visits in eight days. She buys you coffee.

BRENNAN
She asks about the building. I say it's a building.

SECOND BROTHER
What did you tell her about the ward?

BRENNAN
Nothing. I've never been up to the ward.

FIRST BROTHER
(a small pause)
I didn't say the ward.

Brennan understands the shape of the evening at exactly the same moment the first punch arrives — not wild, not angry; the efficient, practised kind, aimed at the body so the face stays presentable.

BRENNAN
(going down)
I don't know anything—

FIRST BROTHER
(kneeling beside him, reasonable)
You have a key. You have a key to a lift that goes to a floor. That's all we need to know, and we already know it, so you can stop.

**INT. HUB — STAIRWELL — TWENTY MINUTES LATER**

They leave him sitting against the wall with his arms around his knees, breathing carefully. The stairwell light clicks off on its timer and comes back on when he moves.

**INT. ST. ALDWYN'S — CORRIDOR — 1:20 A.M.**

Iris and AOIFE outside a treatment room. Brennan's jaw is wired. He is asleep.

IRIS
(watching him)
They took his phone.

AOIFE
They took his phone, his keys, and the shift rota from the wall. It's not about what he knows. It's about who he could tell.

IRIS
Who did it?

AOIFE
(a beat)
I know them. The one on the left takes the collection at the eight o'clock. He has a sister in Leeds and he sends her money.

IRIS
And they're still in the house.

AOIFE
They're still in the house.

IRIS
And you're still going to tell me the Order is load-bearing.

AOIFE
(flaring, for the first time)
I never said the men were. I said the *Bell* was. Don't make me defend the men, Iris. I've spent my whole life not defending the men.

IRIS
(a beat)
I'm sorry.

AOIFE
Don't be sorry. Be *fast*. They're frightened now, and frightened men in that house do worse than this. They'll go to the armory next, and then they'll stand outside it, and then somebody will decide the armory is a solution.

IRIS
What's in the armory?

AOIFE
(looking at Brennan)
You should see it.

IRIS
Why are you doing this?

AOIFE
Because I've typed nine transfers for a man who should have been in a police station, and every one of them was signed and filed and *correct*, and he's still in a house with children in it.

(beat)

I want to be on the other side of a door for once.

**INT. ST. ALDWYN'S — CORRIDOR — CONTINUOUS**

Down the hall, past the lifts, Ward Nine's door is propped open for the night staff. The boy's drawing is still on the bed. It has been put up on the wall above the bed with tape, by someone, that evening.

Iris doesn't see it. She is already gone.
# CHAPTER 17 — THE ARMORY

**EXT. NINTH HOUSE — CLOISTER — 2:10 A.M.**

A courtyard with a covered walk, eleven arches, rain coming off the roof in strings. No lights but the votive stand.

AOIFE walks ahead with a torch held low. Iris behind her with the flight case she has not put down in three days.

IRIS
How many people know about this door?

AOIFE
Four. The Bishop, the steward, me, and a man in Doncaster who is ninety-one.

IRIS
And the men who beat Brennan?

AOIFE
(a beat)
Five.

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — THE BELL CHAMBER — CONTINUOUS**

The chapel of the ninth hour: cold stone, eight hundred candles in racks, and at the end, on a plinth of black marble, the Bell.

Two metres of bronze in a gold sheath, mouth down, silent. Four hundred years of it. The candlelight moves on the gold and the gold does not answer.

Iris stops in the doorway. She does not go to it. She looks at it the way you look at a dog that has bitten someone in your family.

AOIFE
(quietly)
It's not the Bell that did it. It's the men who sold the pieces.

IRIS
I know what it is.

AOIFE
Do you?

IRIS
It's a lump of metal with a story in it. The story is the expensive part.

AOIFE
(a small, tired smile)
You did grow up here.

She goes past the Bell without looking at it — she has looked at it every day for nine years — to the wall behind the plinth. A steel door, painted the same cream as the stone, with a keypad and an iron keyhole.

Two keys: the padlock, and the iron one.

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — THE ARMORY — CONTINUOUS**

Fluorescent tubes stutter on. The room smells of oil and old cardboard.

Racks. Forty carbines. Body armour in three sizes. A crate of stun grenades with a municipal inventory tag still on the side, and a delivery note in a plastic sleeve.

IRIS
(read, flat)
"City of — Depot, Sector 4. Twelve units. Signed for."

AOIFE
They buy from the same depot as the police. It's legal. It's all legal. That's the trick of it — nobody in this building has ever broken a law that would put them in a cell.

IRIS
And the forty men?

AOIFE
Are a *heritage security detail.*
(off Iris's look)
I didn't write the paperwork. I only filed it. I filed it in 2019 and I filed it correctly.

On the wall: a framed photograph. Forty men in grey coats on the steps of the house, 2019, the Bishop seated in the middle.

Iris takes out her phone.

AOIFE
Not yet.

IRIS
Aoife—

AOIFE
If you take that picture now, they'll know the key was used. They'll change every lock in the house, move the file, and by Thursday the armory will be a room with a carpet in it.

IRIS
Then when?

AOIFE
When it can't be undone.

IRIS
That's a beautiful sentence and it means nothing.

AOIFE
It means: not while they can still tidy up.

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — THE ARMORY — DESK — CONTINUOUS**

A grey steel desk. A ring binder, tabbed. Iris opens it at random and finds a name.

IRIS
(reading)
"Brother Cael. Transferred: Ninth House to St. Brannock's, 2011. St. Brannock's to the Low Quarter Mission, 2014. Low Quarter to the coast house, 2017."

She turns the page. A photograph, stapled: a man in his fifties, mild face, glasses.

IRIS (CONT'D)
Nine houses. Nine years.

AOIFE
(not looking at her)
Nine.

IRIS
What did he do?

AOIFE
What do you think he did.

Iris closes the binder. She is very still, and her hands are not shaking, which is worse.

IRIS
And you kept him.

AOIFE
The Bishop kept him. I filed the transfers. I was twenty-three and I typed them, and I told myself the file was a kind of justice because at least it *existed.*

(beat)

It wasn't.

IRIS
Where is he now?

AOIFE
The coast house. There are four brothers there and a cook who is eighty.

IRIS
And the children?

AOIFE
There are no children. There have been no children since 2018, because the last time it happened the family went to the police and the police went to the Bishop and the Bishop moved him again.

IRIS
That's not nothing.

AOIFE
No. It's nine years of nothing with a signature on every page.

Iris looks at the carbines, the armour, the crate with a municipal tag.

IRIS
Why are there forty rifles in a house of prayer?

AOIFE
Because in 2018 a man came to the door with a hammer and a grievance, and the Bishop decided that the next time, the door would be held by men.

(beat)

And because a frightened old man with a hundred staff and a relic will always, always end up buying guns.

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — CLOISTER — 2:40 A.M.**

They come back through the arches. The rain has stopped.

IRIS
He'll see us on a camera.

AOIFE
There are no cameras in the cloister.

IRIS
Because?

AOIFE
(a beat)
Because I turned them off in 2019.

Iris stops walking.

IRIS
Why?

AOIFE
Because I wanted to be able to do this without being watched.

(beat)

I've been waiting six years for somebody to come and ask me to open that door. You're not the first. You're the first I said yes to.

# CHAPTER 18 — VALERIUS

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — BISHOP'S STUDY — 3:05 A.M.**

A warm room: books to the ceiling, a fire banked for the night, a window over the courtyard where the votive stand is still burning.

BISHOP VALERIUS (67) is awake, dressed, and unsurprised. He pours two glasses of water, because he does not drink, and sets one in front of her.

VALERIUS
You left at nineteen. You wrote me a letter.

IRIS
I called your house a machine.

VALERIUS
You called it a machine that eats the young. It was a good letter. I've quoted it.

IRIS
(to the water)
I'm here about a room with forty rifles in it.

VALERIUS
(without flinching)
I know.

IRIS
And about Brother Cael.

Valerius sets his glass down.

VALERIUS
Yes.

IRIS
That's it? Yes?

VALERIUS
What would you like? A denial? I have made three. They were all lies and they all worked, and I stopped being able to say them in 2017.

IRIS
Then why is he still in a house with a cook in it?

VALERIUS
Because the alternative was the police.

IRIS
That's the *point* of the police.

VALERIUS
And the police meant the papers, and the papers meant the Bell.

(beat)

Nine hundred people come through this house in a week, Iris. They come because they cannot sleep. They come because their children are ill, or their marriages are over, or they have done something that they cannot say out loud in a room with a light on. They kneel in that chapel at four in the morning and they are not alone, and then they go to work.

If the Bell becomes a scandal, it stops being load-bearing. And then they have nowhere to go and nothing to kneel to at four in the morning, and the men who sell them the pieces will simply move to another town and sell them there.

IRIS
You weighed a child against a lump of gold.

VALERIUS
I weighed a city against a man. I was wrong, and I did it anyway, and I will answer for it. But not to a man who is turning minds off with a machine and calling it hygiene.

**INT. BISHOP'S STUDY — CONTINUOUS**

He goes to the desk, unlocks a drawer, and takes out a card — a mass card, printed, with a name written in biro.

VALERIUS
Nell Kalu. 2016. The inquest said malnutrition.

Iris does not move.

VALERIUS (CONT'D)
I went to the funeral. You didn't see me. I stood at the back and I paid for the flowers and I went home and I did nothing at all, because the man who sold her the fragment had done it in a tent in a field with a receipt, and there was no law I could use that would not have taken the Bell down with him.

IRIS
(very quietly)
You could have taken the Bell down.

VALERIUS
Yes.

(beat)

I could have. That is the whole of my confession, and it will not fit on a form.

**INT. BISHOP'S STUDY — LATER**

The fire has burned down. Iris has not touched the water.

IRIS
I need the armory. Not the rifles — the record of them. The tags, the delivery notes, the security detail paperwork. All of it.

VALERIUS
For the police?

IRIS
For a courtroom, six months from now, when nobody is frightened any more and everybody has a theory about what happened in the square.

VALERIUS
(a long look)
And the Bell?

IRIS
The Bell goes into a room with a judge in it and a very long caption on the wall.

VALERIUS
Then no.

IRIS
You just confessed to me.

VALERIUS
I confessed to *you.* Not to a commission that will turn four hundred years into a paragraph about a relic shop.

(beat)

You want my armory? Take it — I'll open it myself in daylight, with a lawyer and a camera, and I'll say every word you just heard. On one condition: the Bell survives the week unbroken, and the people who kneel to it are allowed to keep kneeling.

IRIS
I can't promise you that.

VALERIUS
(nodding, almost kindly)
Then we are both people who have decided what a city is worth, and the only difference between us is which week we decided it in.

**INT. NINTH HOUSE — CORRIDOR — 3:40 A.M.**

Aoife waits at the end of the corridor. Iris comes out and walks past her without stopping.

AOIFE
(following)
What did he say?

IRIS
He said yes and then he said no.

AOIFE
That's the whole man, that.

IRIS
(stopping)
He knew about Nell.

AOIFE
(quietly)
He says her name in the mass intentions every November. He's done it for nine years. I type the list.

Iris looks at her.

IRIS
That's not the same as doing something.

AOIFE
No.

(beat)
