Book One Hundred

The Navigator of Meluhha

Five thousand years ago a sailor known only by the cargo he carried took one last load across the sea — and found, at the far end of it, the one thing he had never thought to ask for.

Chapter One

The Last Manifest

The warehouse at Ur smelled of bitumen, wet rope and the sour breath of a hundred men who had been loading a ship since before dawn. Ilum stood at the top of the plank and watched them work, and did the arithmetic he had been doing for thirty years: how much a hull could carry, how much the river would steal, how much the sea would take for itself and never give back.

He was forty-one years old. His beard had gone the colour of old rope and his left shoulder ached in the season of the north wind. He had crossed to Magan nineteen times and to Meluhha eleven, and he had never lost a ship. That was the whole of his reputation, and it was enough for a man to live on, and it was the reason the temple clerk was standing in front of him now with a waxed tablet and a face like a closed door.

"One more," the clerk said. "The Lady of the Granary asks it personally."

"The Lady of the Granary can ask the river."

"She asked the river. The river gave her a drought." The clerk turned the tablet so Ilum could see the numbers, though Ilum could not read them well and both of them knew it. "Carnelian. Ivory. Cotton in bales. Two hundred jars of oil for the temple of Dilmun." He paused on the last line in a way that was meant to be noticed. "And one passenger."

Ilum looked down at the deck, where his crew of fourteen were arguing about how to stow a crate of hardwood so it would not crush the ivory beneath it. Besu was among them, moving slower than the others, his hand on the rail more often than on the rope. Besu had taught Ilum the stars when Ilum was nine years old and had been dying, quietly and with great patience, for two years now.

"A passenger," Ilum said.

"A woman. From the east. She pays in tin."

Tin was the one thing a Mesopotamian port could never have enough of and never admit it needed. Ilum watched a heron lift off the canal wall and go north, unhurried, the way a bird goes when it has nothing to prove to anyone. Then he said the thing he had said eleven times before and meant less each time.

"We sail at the new moon."

He found Besu at the stern, sitting on a coil of rope with his eyes closed. The old man's chest rose and fell like something the tide had left behind.

"You should be in bed," Ilum said.

"I should be in Meluhha." Besu opened one eye. "I have a jar of my own to deliver. My mother's family is from the river with the five mouths. I promised her I would take her home, and I have been not taking her home for thirty years."

"Then come. But you will not pull an oar."

"I have not pulled an oar since your beard was black." Besu shifted, and something in his chest made a sound that was not a word. "Ilum. When we get there, you will take the jar up the river yourself. I want to see it done, not hear about it."

Ilum said yes, because it cost nothing to say yes on the dock. That is the great lie of every voyage: on the dock, everything is still possible.

He walked home along the canal as the light went the colour of beer. Ana was on the roof, where she always was at that hour, doing nothing at all except looking at the sky as if it owed her money.

"You said the last one was the last one," she said, without turning.

"I said the last one was the one before the last one."

"You are a poor liar and a worse husband." She held out her hand and he took it, and her palm was warm and dry and full of the smell of indigo from the vats below. "How long?"

"Four months if the wind is kind."

"And if it is not?"

He did not answer that, because for thirty years he had never had to. She turned then and looked at him properly, and he saw that she was not angry. That was worse. She was counting.

"Four months," she said. "Then I will believe you."

Chapter Two

The Water Road

The Black Reed went down the channel on the flood and out past the reed beds where the water turned from brown to green to a blue so hard it looked like a thing you could cut. Ilum stood at the steering oar with his feet apart and let the ship find herself, the way a man lets a horse find its own footing on a bad road.

She was forty cubits of sewn teak, the planks drilled and lashed with coir rope and caulked with bitumen, because nails in salt water are a slow suicide. Her sail was a mat of woven reed, square and heavy, and when the wind got into it she groaned like a granary door. Ilum had sailed her for eleven years and he trusted her more than he trusted most people, which he considered a reasonable position.

The passenger came up from the little reed shelter they had built her in the bows on the second morning. She was not what he had expected. She was older than he was, thin as a mast, with grey in her braided hair and a leather case strapped across her back that she would not let anyone touch. She stood at the rail for a long time looking at the water, and then she said, without introduction:

"You are steering three points off the star."

Ilum looked at the sky. The sun was still high and useless. "You cannot see the star. It is morning."

"I can see where it will be. It is a habit." She turned. Her eyes were the colour of canal water. "My name is Velli. I read the sky for the temple at Harappa, and before that for my father, and before that for no one at all, which was the best of the three."

"You paid in tin to be a passenger. Not to be a pilot."

"I paid in tin to be left alone." She smiled, briefly, and it changed her whole face. "I am telling you about the star because I do not wish to drown. It is not a criticism."

He gave the oar to his cousin and went forward and looked where she pointed, and the thing she said was true. He corrected two points and said nothing about it, and she said nothing about it, and that was the beginning of what passed between them.

At night the sea went black and the sky came down close and the crew slept in the lee of the cargo, and Ilum stayed awake with the star. He had learned it from Besu, who had learned it from a man from Dilmun who had learned it from no one, and the whole of navigation was this: find the one thing in the sky that does not move, and hold it at the angle your father told you, and trust that the world is round enough to be kind.

Somewhere on the ninth night he found himself thinking about Ana's hands. Not the rest of her — her hands, the way they moved when she lifted a skein of dyed wool out of the vat and held it up against the light to see if the colour had taken. He had watched her do that a thousand times and had never once told her it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen a person do. He resolved to tell her. He resolved this with the particular sincerity of a man alone at an oar, which is the least reliable kind.

On the twelfth day they raised land: a low brown line, like a stain on the rim of the world. Magan. The copper hills. The place where the sea stopped pretending.

They came in on the evening tide, and there on the beach, waiting, were eleven men with copper-bladed spears and a man in a linen wrap who was smiling in a way that Ilum had learned, over thirty years, to price very carefully.

Chapter Three

What the Manifest Did Not Say

The man in the linen wrap was called Rimush and he had authority of a kind that does not come from a temple. He walked the deck of the Black Reed twice, tapping crates with his knuckles, and he did not look at the manifest once.

"Two hundred jars of oil," he said.

"For the temple at Dilmun."

"The temple at Dilmun has been waiting for those jars since the flood before last." Rimush stopped in front of the passenger's shelter and looked at it for a while. "And this."

"A woman from the east. She paid in tin."

"She paid in tin," Rimush agreed, and something moved behind his face. "Put half the tin ashore tonight, navigator. The other half when you leave Magan. That is the toll now."

Ilum had paid tolls at Magan for twenty years and the toll had never been half a cargo's worth of tin. He looked at the eleven spears on the beach and did the arithmetic he was good at. Then he looked at the jars of oil and did the arithmetic he was better at.

"Half the tin is the whole profit," he said.

"Then it is a good thing you love the sea and not the profit." Rimush smiled again. "You are carrying something else, navigator. I can smell it. A ship that has been loaded by a temple clerk always carries something the clerk did not write down."

Ilum said nothing, because there was nothing to say that would not be a confirmation. But that night he took a lamp into the hold and went through the crates himself, and behind the bales of cotton, in a box of Meluhhan hardwood that should have held twelve planks and held nine, he found a jar of grey clay sealed with a stamped bulla, and around the jar, packed in dry straw, forty small blades of a metal that was not copper and not tin and did not come from any hill he knew.

He carried one blade up to the deck. Velli was awake, as she always was, sitting against the mast with her leather case on her knees.

"Iron," she said, before he asked. "It falls from the sky, sometimes. In the desert east of my river there is a whole hill of it, and the smiths there have learned to work it, and the temple does not want anyone to know." She looked at the blade in his hand. "That is what I am carrying too. Not the metal. The knowledge of where it comes from, and how it is made, and what it will do to every copper mine between here and the mountains."

"You are carrying a war," Ilum said.

"I am carrying a plough that can also be a sword. The war is what men do with it." She held out her hand and he gave her the blade, and she weighed it in her palm like a woman weighing grain. "Put it back. In the morning, give Rimush his half of the tin, and smile, and sail."

"And if he searches the hold?"

"Then he finds nine planks and a jar of clay." She gave the blade back. "He is not looking for iron, navigator. He is looking for a reason to take the ship. Do not give him one."

In the morning Ilum gave Rimush half the tin and smiled, and Rimush, who had expected an argument and had prepared for one, found himself with nothing to do with his eleven spears. The Black Reed went out on the ebb with the whole crew breathing again and the copper hills going brown behind them.

Besu had not come up on deck for two days. When Ilum went down to him he was lying in the dark with his eyes open and his hand closed around the neck of a small sealed jar.

"You paid the toll," the old man whispered.

"I paid it."

"Good. Never fight a man on his own beach." Besu's grip tightened. "Ilum. When you take her up the river. Do not open the jar. There is nothing in it but ash and a promise, and a promise is not improved by being looked at."

Chapter Four

The Long Night

The storm came out of the south-west on the twenty-first day, and it came without the usual decency of a darkening sky. One moment the sea was the colour of a knife and the next it was a wall, and the Black Reed went up it like a beetle on a step and came down into a hole in the world.

Ilum had been in eleven storms. He remembered all of them the way a man remembers the faces of people who tried to kill him. This one was different in one respect: it lasted.

They took the sail down and lost two men doing it, not to the water but to the yard, which swung and caught one across the chest and threw the other into the sea, and the sea kept him. They ran before the wind under bare poles with four men on the steering oars and the rest bailing with their hands. Ilum lashed himself to the mast and put his face into the rain and shouted things at the crew that he would not remember afterwards.

Somewhere in the middle of it Besu came on deck.

Ilum saw him and screamed at him to go below, and the old man did not hear, or did not choose to. He stood at the rail with the jar in his arms, and he was looking at the water with an expression Ilum had never seen on him before, which was not fear. It was recognition.

The wave that took him was not the biggest of the night. It came over the quarter and the deck went white and when the water ran off, Besu was gone, and the jar was gone, and the rail where his hand had been was splintered as if something had bitten it.

They found nothing. You never find anything.

Ilum stayed at the mast until the storm wore itself out, which took the rest of the night, and when the sun came up the sea was flat and stupid and innocent, the way the sea always is afterwards. He counted the crew. Twelve. He counted the cargo. Most of it. He sat down on the deck and could not make his hands stop shaking, and Velli came and sat beside him and said nothing for a long time.

"He was your father," she said at last.

"He was my teacher."

"That is what a father is, when the other one is dead."

Ilum looked at the water. Twenty years ago he had taken his brother Kesh on his first crossing, and Kesh had been seventeen and had laughed at everything, and on the eighth night Kesh had gone forward to piss over the side in a calm sea and had not come back, and they had searched for four hours with lamps, and the sea had given them nothing but a shoe.

"I have lost two men to this water," Ilum said. "And now a third. And the water does not even know."

"No," said Velli. "It does not. That is the thing you have to decide about, and you have to decide it before you are old, or it will eat you the way it ate him." She stood up, her knees cracking. "He went over the side holding a promise. He did not go over the side holding a grievance. There is a difference, and it is the only difference there is."

They sailed on. The wind came round to the east and then to the north-east, and the star came out at night, and Ilum held it at the angle Besu had taught him, and by the fifteenth morning after the storm there was land ahead, and the land was green.

Chapter Five

The River with the Five Mouths

Meluhha was the greenest thing Ilum had ever seen and it made him uneasy. He had spent his life on water the colour of lead and land the colour of bread; here the river came down out of the hills and split into five mouths, and every bank was so thick with growing that the air itself tasted of it, sweet and heavy, like the inside of a fruit.

The port was a city of brick and dust and a great many people who did not look up when a Mesopotamian ship came in, which was, Ilum thought, the most sophisticated thing he had ever seen a city do.

He sold the carnelian. He sold the ivory at a price that made his cousin's eyes go wide. He delivered the cotton and took on a cargo of teak and tin and peacocks for the return, and he paid the harbour clerk in barley, and he did all of it on four hours of sleep a night.

On the sixth day he hired a boat and went up the river with the jar.

He had found it. That was the thing he had not expected. On the morning after the storm, going through the wreckage in the forepeak, he had found Besu's other jar — the small one, the one with his mother's ash inside — wedged under a broken plank, undamaged, the seal unbroken. He had sat on the deck with it in his lap for a long time.

Velli came with him up the river. She had business of her own in the east, she said, and no hurry to do it, and a boat with two people in it goes faster than a boat with one, which was a lie and both of them knew it.

The river was slow and the light through the trees came down in pieces. They passed villages where the children ran to the bank and shouted something he did not understand, and men standing in the water up to their chests with nets, and once, at dusk, an animal the size of a house that came down to drink and looked at them with the calm contempt of a thing that has never been hunted.

On the third evening she asked him about Ana.

He told her about the vats, and the way indigo stained the skin of the wrists and never came out, and how she had once sold a length of cloth to a temple steward for twice what he had offered and then given half of it to the steward's own servant, in front of him, without a word. He told her about the roof, and the hour before dark, and the way Ana looked at the sky as though it owed her money.

"You have been married nineteen years," Velli said.

"Twenty in the spring."

"And you still talk about her hands." She was quiet a moment. "Most men your age talk about their sons, or their debts, or their knees. You talk about her hands." She trailed her fingers in the water. "You are a very rich man, navigator, and you have no idea."

He looked at her, and she looked back, and the boat went on down the brown water, and neither of them said anything for a while. There was a moment — there is always a moment — when the evening was warm and the river was slow and she was, after all, a woman who had been alone a long time.

Ilum took up the oar instead.

"Two more days," he said. "Then I will have kept my promise."

Velli smiled at the water. "Yes," she said. "You will."

Chapter Six

What Was in the Jar

The village was called something Ilum could not pronounce and did not try to. It stood where a smaller river met the great one, and it had eleven houses, a well, a threshing floor, and a woman of about eighty who was sitting on a wall when they arrived and who looked at Ilum's jar and said, in a dialect so old it was almost Akkadian:

"That is Besu's mother's jar."

"You knew him."

"I was twelve when he left. He was going to be a great man in the west and send for us." She spat, without malice, into the dust. "He was a great man in the west. He did not send for us. Sit down. You have carried that jar a long way and you look like a man who has been carrying it."

He sat. She gave him water and then beer, and she asked him questions in the way that old people do, out of order, and he answered them all honestly, and when he had finished telling her about the storm she was quiet for a long time.

"So he is in the sea," she said.

"He is in the sea."

"He always said he would be." She looked at the jar on the ground between them. "He used to tell me the sea keeps everything, and I used to tell him the sea gives nothing back, and we were both right, and now it is forty years later and here is a stranger with his mother in a jar." She nodded at it. "Open it."

"He said not to."

"He is dead, and I am not, and I am his cousin." She said it with a finality that Ilum recognised from twenty years of marriage. He broke the seal.

There was ash inside, as Besu had said. There was also something that was not ash: a small square of fired clay, a tablet the size of his thumb, with marks cut into it. Ilum could not read them well, but he could read enough. It was not a letter. It was a list — a list of names, nine of them, and beside each name a village, and beside each village a number.

"That is the tithe record," the old woman said, and her voice had changed. "That is the record the temple burned when they took the granaries. He kept a copy. He kept it for forty years in a jar with his mother and he brought it home." She took the tablet and held it in both hands, and then she began to laugh, and then she was not laughing.

They buried the ash that evening under the tree by the well, and the whole village came, and nobody said anything formal. The old woman poured beer on the ground and said, "He came back," and that was the whole of the ceremony.

Velli stood at the back with her leather case on her back and watched.

"You see," she said afterwards, as they walked back to the boat. "It was never a jar of ash. It was a man's whole life, wrapped up small enough to carry, and he could not bring himself to look at it, so he asked someone else to carry it to the end." She looked at the river. "That is what a navigator is for, in the end. Not the cargo. The carrying."

Ilum went back down the river with an empty boat and a lighter chest, and the green went past him on both sides, and for the first time in thirty years he was in no hurry at all.

Chapter Seven

The Harbor of Water

The return took fifty-one days, which was nine more than the wind owed him, and by the last week Ilum had stopped counting at all. That was new. He had counted the days of every crossing of his life and on this one, somewhere off Dilmun, he had simply stopped, and had found that the sea did not become less interesting for it.

He came up the channel at Ur on the evening tide, and the mud was the same mud and the warehouses were the same warehouses and the heron was standing in the same place on the canal wall, or its grandson was, and Ilum stood at the steering oar with his shoulder aching and felt his chest fill up like a sail.

Ana was not on the roof.

He went home with his bag on his shoulder, up the lane, past the vats, and the door was open, and she was inside, standing at the table with her back to him, doing the sums for a merchant from Nippur. She did not turn around. She said, to the merchant:

"My husband comes back from the sea at dusk and stands in the door like a beggar instead of saying my name. You will excuse him. He has been among foreigners."

The merchant, who was a sensible man, gathered his tablets and left.

She turned around then, and looked at him for a long moment, from his ruined shoulder to the grey in his beard to the sand still in his sandals, and her eyes were wet and her mouth was not.

"Fifty-one days," she said. "I counted."

"I stopped counting."

"Then you are finally learning something." She crossed the room and put both hands flat on his chest, and pushed him, once, hard enough that he had to take a step back. "Nineteen years I have stood on that roof at dusk for a man who said four months. Four months. Not five."

"The wind—"

"Do not tell me about the wind." And then she was against him with her face in his neck and her hands in his hair, and he held her and did not say anything at all for a long time, because he had learned at last that there are moments a man should not talk through.

Later, when the lamp had burned down and the house had gone quiet and the last cart had gone past in the lane, she lay against his shoulder with her hand spread flat over his heart, and he watched it rise and fall and thought that this was the only chart he had ever needed.

"What happened to you out there," she said. It was not a question.

He told her. Besu, and the storm, and the jar, and the village with eleven houses, and the old woman laughing in the dust. He told her about the river that split into five mouths and the animal the size of a house. He told her about Velli, and the star, and the iron that falls out of the sky.

She was quiet for a while. Then she said: "Did you sleep with her?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because she was going somewhere and I was going home," he said, "and I have spent thirty years carrying other men's cargo. I did not want to carry that home to you."

Ana was silent for so long that he thought she had gone to sleep. Then she turned her face up and kissed him, unhurried, the way she did everything, and drew the blanket up over the two of them, and the night closed over the house the way it closes over a harbour.

* * *

He did not sail again. He sold his share of the Black Reed to his cousin and bought a share of a dye works instead, and for the next four years he was home every evening at dusk, and Ana never once said anything about the roof, because she did not need to sit on it alone any more.

In the spring of the fourth year a boat came up the channel with a letter in a leather case, addressed to the navigator of the Black Reed. It was from Velli, and it was very short, and it said that the hill of iron in the desert east of her river had become a market, and that the smiths were selling ploughs, and that the temple had burned her house, and that she was alive and had been right.

Ilum read it twice and put it in the box with the thumb-tablet the temple clerk had given him the morning he signed for one more crossing. Then he went up onto the roof, where Ana was, doing nothing at all except looking at the sky as if it owed her money.

"A good voyage?" she said, without turning.

"The best I ever made," he said. "And I have finally finished it."

The books behind the book

Meluhha is the name Mesopotamian scribes gave the Indus civilisation, and the sea roads between Ur, Dilmun, Magan and Meluhha were carrying carnelian, copper, tin and timber five thousand years ago — before any of the nations we now argue about existed. The voyage, the storm and the people in this story are imagined; the sea road is not.
