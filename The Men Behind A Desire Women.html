The Men Behind A Desire Women

by Amim Ahsan · writing under the pen name Amimul Ahsan

Every empire begins with a confession.

John is twenty-three, hungry, and writing in a room with one bulb when a small publisher named Jackson offers him ten percent and a chance. The novel he writes becomes the confession a city reads on buses and at traffic lights, and the first thing it buys him is the power to control the paper it is printed on. What follows is a publishing empire built in Motijheel, a marriage tested by an honest speech given to the wrong room, and a long walk back to the only thing that was ever worth keeping: a sentence that is true.

Act I · Ink & Hunger · Chapter OneThe Men Behind A Desire Women

The Ink and the Ashes

November. Room 113, a lamp, and a phantom caller.

✦

The fluorescent tube above the desk flickered with a faint, high-pitched hum, the sound of a mosquito trapped behind glass. It was three in the morning in Dhaka, and the city outside Room 113 had gone quiet in the way a city only goes quiet when it is holding its breath.

John sat on the edge of the bed, a notebook open on his knees, a pen in his hand that had not moved in twenty minutes. The page was blank except for a single line at the top, written in handwriting that looked like someone else's: Man Behind One His Desire Women.

He had written the title first, before he had written a single word of the story, because the title was the only part he was sure of. The rest of it was a fog. The rest of it was the smell of boiled rice from the corridor, the ceiling fan turning with a click that came around every two seconds, the damp patch on the wall shaped vaguely like a map of a country that did not exist.

The phone rang.

Nobody called at three in the morning unless something had happened. John looked at the screen, at the unknown number, and let it ring twice more before he picked up. A man's voice, older, careful, with the polished flatness of someone who had made this call many times before.

"Is this the author of Man Behind One His Desire Women?"

John almost laughed. "I'm the person who typed it," he said.

"I'm Jackson," the voice said. "I run a small press. I read your manuscript on a train from Chittagong to Dhaka, all two hundred and forty pages of it, and I have not stopped thinking about it since. I want to publish it."

John looked at the blank page in his lap. The pen was still in his hand. Outside, a dog barked somewhere in the dark, and a rickshaw bell answered it, thin and far away.

"At what percent?" John asked.

The line was quiet for a moment. "Ten," Jackson said.

"Ten," John repeated.

"Ten percent of the cover price, on every copy sold, paid quarterly. I know it isn't much. It is what I can offer. I am not a big house. I am one man and a rented press and a list of eleven authors who trust me."

John closed his eyes. He was twenty-three years old, he had four hundred taka in his wallet, and he had been eating rice and lentils for eleven days because the money his father sent each month had stopped arriving and he had been too proud to ask why.

"Then publish it," he said.

"Don't you want to know the terms? The print run? The date?"

"No," John said. "I want to know that someone read it."

The line was quiet again. Then Jackson said, in a different voice, softer, almost sad, "I read it twice. The part where the boy carries his mother's sewing machine through the flood. Did that happen?"

The pen moved in John's hand then, a small unconscious circle on the corner of the page, like a signature, like a wound. "Everything in it happened," he said. "That's the problem."

After he hung up, he sat for a long time in the dark, listening to the ceiling fan. The lamp was still on. The notebook was still open. The title was still there, and beneath it, where the story was supposed to go, the paper was white and clean and awful in its patience.

He wrote one sentence. Then another. Then he crossed out the first.

At four in the morning, the call to prayer rose from the mosque at the end of the lane, and John put down the pen, and went out onto the small balcony, and stood in the cold with his hands on the railing, watching the sky turn from black to a color he had no name for.

He was going to be published. He was going to be a writer. He was going to be somebody.

He did not know, standing there in the cold, that the book would sell eleven thousand copies in its first year, and that Jackson would call him again in the spring, and that by the following winter John would know exactly how much a man's soul weighed, because he would have carried his own to the market and sold it, and he would have gotten a very good price.

For now there was only the cold, and the fan, and the blank page.

And the title, patient as a creditor.

The Weight of Unpaid Tea

A contract, a tea stall, and the arithmetic of shame.

✦

The advance was five thousand taka. Jackson sent it by bKash on a Thursday afternoon, with a message that said: Against royalties. Do not spend it all on one thing.

John read the message four times and then laughed out loud in the empty room, because five thousand taka was not a sum of money to him; it was a door. He could pay the rent. He could buy a new bulb for the corridor. He could eat something that had not been boiled in the same pot as yesterday's something.

He did none of those things. He walked to the tea stall at the corner of the lane and sat down on the bench and ordered a cup of tea, and then another, and then a plate of singara, and he paid for the man at the next table who had been arguing with the stall owner about eleven taka since before John arrived.

The stall owner's name was Karim. He was fifty-eight years old, he had a mustache like a comma, and he had known John since John was a first-year student who could not afford milk in his tea.

"You look like a man who has won something," Karim said, wiping the counter with a cloth that had given up.

"I have," John said. "A publisher."

Karim stopped wiping. "A publisher," he repeated, in the tone of a man being told about a relative who has married a foreigner.

"A small one. His name is Jackson. He read the book."

"The book." Karim looked at him for a long moment. "Your book. The one you sit here and write in the mornings when you should be at your classes."

"That one."

Karim put down the cloth. He poured a fresh cup of tea without being asked and set it in front of John, and then he leaned on the counter with both elbows, which meant he had something to say that would take a while.

"Listen to me," he said. "My father had a shop in Old Dhaka. Cloth. Good cloth. For thirty years, everyone in that lane bought their cloth from my father, and everyone said, Karim's father, what a man, what a shop. Then my father died, and the shop was mine, and I discovered that everyone who had said those things had also been taking cloth on credit for thirty years. Not stealing. Never stealing. Just not paying. And I could not ask them for it, because they were my father's friends, my uncles, my neighbors, men who had held me as a baby."

He picked up the cloth again and folded it into a square.

"That is what a debt is," Karim said. "It is not a number. It is a person's face. You will learn this now, with your publisher, or you will learn it later, with your readers. Either way, you will learn it."

John drank his tea. It was too sweet. It was always too sweet. He had never once told Karim.

"I'll be careful," he said.

"No," Karim said. "You won't. Nobody is careful at your age. But come and sit here when it goes wrong, and I will give you tea, and you will not have to pay for it."

John laughed. But walking home, he took out his phone and opened the agreement Jackson had emailed, and read the ten percent again, and read the part about the print run of two thousand copies, and read the part about the author's copies, which were three.

He stood in the corridor outside Room 113 for a moment, key in hand, and did the arithmetic in his head. Two thousand copies. Ten percent. A cover price he could not remember.

Behind him, someone came up the stairs carrying something heavy, and he stepped aside without looking, and he heard a woman say, "You live in 113. You are always awake."

Seema. She lived on the third floor, she studied statistics, she carried her father's old briefcase because it was the only bag she owned that fit her notebooks, and she had asked him once, at this same hour, whether he ever slept.

"I'm awake," John said. "I'm published now. Apparently."

She stopped on the step above him and looked down, and her face was half in shadow and half in the yellow corridor light, and she did the one thing nobody else in his life had done since he had told them the news.

She asked, "Are you sure you want that?"

He should have said yes.

Later, much later, when the presses were his and the paper was his and the city came to him with its cap in its hands, he would remember that he had said yes, and that she had nodded slowly, and gone up the stairs, and closed her door, and that the corridor bulb had hummed in the silence she left behind.

The Shadow of Forty-Five Percent

The first print run and the difference between fame and a receipt.

✦

The first copies arrived in six boxes on the last day of November, and John opened them in the corridor because Room 113 was too small to hold the smell of new paper and hot ink and something like certainty.

The cover was simple: a dark room, a window, a woman's shape in the glass. Jackson had designed it himself, and John had hated it at first and then loved it and then hated it again, which he understood to be how a writer loves everything he has not yet learned to distrust.

He gave one copy to Karim, who kept it under the counter next to the tea and said he would read it on Friday. He gave one to Mizan, his roommate, who read the first chapter and announced that the protagonist was obviously John with a better haircut. He gave one to Seema, who did not read it, or did not say she had.

And then the book was out, in eleven bookshops and one railway stall and a website Jackson had built with his own hands, and nothing happened for two weeks.

Nothing. John checked the sales dashboard every hour, then every morning, then once a day, then with a kind of grim dignity once every three days. Six copies. Nine copies. Fourteen copies. A man from Khulna wrote a review that was three paragraphs long and said the novel was "a fever, and a good one."

Then the second month arrived, and the shipments went out, and the numbers became something else. Sixty. Eighty. One hundred and forty. Jackson called and said the print run had sold through and he was doing another, three thousand this time, and would John please come to the office and sign the papers.

The office was one room above a bakery on Elephant Road. Jackson was not what John had imagined. He was tall and thin and very old, with a white beard that looked like something he had acquired rather than grown, and he wore a watch too big for his wrist and had a habit of holding his pen between two fingers like it might at any moment turn back into a bird and leave.

"The second printing will be four thousand," Jackson said. "I have had to renegotiate with the paper man. The price of paper has gone up. Everything is paper in the end."

John sat across from him. There was a contract on the desk, one page, two columns of small print.

"Ten percent," John said.

"Ten percent," Jackson agreed.

"And the paper man charges you forty-five percent of the cover price."

Jackson's pen stopped. He did not look up. "Who told you that?"

"I know a man at the paper market in Sadarghat. He told me what the mills are charging. I did the arithmetic."

The old man raised his eyes, and for the first time John saw something in them that was not kindly, that had been there all along, in fact, that was the reason the press existed and had survived and would outlive them both.

"You're not a writer," Jackson said. "You're a businessman."

"No," John said. "I'm a hungry writer. There's a difference."

Jackson leaned back. "The difference is what?"

"The businessman would ask for twenty percent," John said. "The writer is asking how much of the cost is mine."

Jackson smiled. It was not a warm smile. "None of it is yours. That is the beauty of a percentage. If the book sells, you eat. If it does not, you starve. And I go on printing."

"Then why did the last print run have a four-page insert that I never approved?"

The pen went still.

"It was a catalogue," Jackson said. "For the press. A short one. Why?"

"No reason," John said. "I'm learning."

He signed the second printing. Three thousand copies. Ten percent. But that night, in Room 113, with the fan clicking and the corridor bulb humming and the city breathing outside, John made the first entry in a notebook he would keep for the next four years.

He wrote: Paper = 45%. Press = 20%. Ink = 6%. Labour = 4%. Jackson = 15%. Me = 10%.

He looked at the numbers for a long time. Then he wrote, underneath, in letters so small they were almost a secret:

Note: the paper is the queen.

Ink on the Pavement

The book finds strangers, and strangers find the author.

✦

The pavement outside the university gate had a book stall that appeared at nine and vanished at six, run by a boy named Rafiq who was nineteen and could sell anything printed, including, once, a manual for a washing machine model that had not been imported since 1984.

John went there on a Tuesday and found his own book on the table, between a pirated medical textbook and a collection of poems by a dead man, and he stood in front of it for so long that Rafiq asked if he needed help.

"How many of these do you sell?" John asked.

Rafiq looked at the cover. "This one? Six, seven a week. A rickshaw puller bought one last Thursday. He read the first page standing at the light and then he paid me and left. He came back Monday and asked if there was another one like it."

"Like it how?"

Rafiq shrugged. "Like a man's own life written down by somebody else."

John bought a copy of his own book that day, at Rafiq's price, without explaining, and walked back to Room 113 with it under his arm. He put it on the shelf next to his mother's photograph and did not open it.

The reviews came in the spring. A newspaper called it "the confession the city had been waiting for." A magazine called it "too much feeling and not enough plot," which John read four times and secretly agreed with. A professor in Chittagong wrote a long essay claiming that the novel's unnamed woman was a symbol of the river, which was so wrong that John laughed for a full minute and then could not stop thinking about it for a week.

But it was the rickshaw puller he remembered. Because the rickshaw puller had not been reading a symbol. He had been reading a story about a boy who carried his mother's sewing machine through the flood, and had recognized the street, and had wept, or had not, and had come back for more.

That was the first hook in him. Not the money, which was still small. Not the reviews, which were kind. The first hook was the knowledge that sentences could enter a man's chest, and settle there, and change the shape of how he breathed.

He tried to explain this to Seema on a Friday, in the corridor, where the third-floor tap had burst and a thin lake of water was making its patient way down the stairs.

"Six or seven a week," he said. "One of them was a rickshaw puller."

"I heard you the first time," Seema said. She was carrying her father's briefcase and wearing a blue shawl, and she was waiting for the water to stop or for someone to come and fix it, whichever came first.

"Isn't it strange? They don't know me. They'll never know me. And they are reading about my mother's sewing machine."

"It isn't yours anymore," Seema said.

"What?"

"The sewing machine. The flood. The boy. You sold it to them. That's what publishing is." She said it without cruelty, the way she might have explained a statistical theorem, and then, because she was not a cruel person, she added, "It isn't a bad thing. It's just a real thing."

John looked at the water on the floor. "You haven't read it."

"I have."

He looked up.

"All of it. Twice." She pulled the shawl tighter. "The part where the mother says she does not need the machine, that she has already lost it and kept the important half. That part is true. I don't know a woman who would not say it exactly like that."

"Including you?"

Seema picked up her briefcase. "Especially me," she said, and went up the stairs, through the water, without getting her shoes wet, which he had never been able to do.

The River of Rice and Cellulose

Sadarghat, a mill owner, and the first bargain.

✦

The paper market at Sadarghat began at four in the morning, when the barges came in from the mills upriver and the wholesale men gathered on the wet stone steps with their hands in their pockets.

John went there alone, wearing the only shirt he owned that had no stains, carrying a notebook and a pen and the five thousand taka Jackson had advanced him against the second printing.

He had spent three weeks learning the words. He knew that paper was measured in reams and in the weight of a thousand sheets, that the difference between forty-two GSM and fifty-eight GSM was the difference between a book that felt like a prayer and a book that felt like a brochure, and that bulk bought at the right hour on the right morning could be sold at the right hour on the right morning to a man who had underestimated his print run.

The mill owner was a widow named Mrs. Chowdhury, and she had inherited the mill from her husband and the mill's debts from her husband's brother, and she did not smile at John when he sat down across from her at the tea shop.

"You are the writer," she said.

"Yes."

"You want paper cheaper than Jackson gives you."

"Yes."

"Why would I sell to you instead of him?"

John put his notebook on the table. "Because Jackson buys four thousand reams a year, and I've looked at his orders, and three of them arrive eleven days late. Because you are running at seventy percent capacity and your warehouse in Narayanganj is holding stock for a buyer in Khulna who is disputing the invoice. Because you have two hundred reams of forty-five GSM in the godown behind this market that has been rained on, and you cannot sell it at full price, and I can."

Mrs. Chowdhury looked at him for a long time. The tea arrived. She did not touch it.

"How do you know about the godown?"

"I walked around it. The back wall is wet. I asked a man who loads trucks."

"What do you want?"

"I want the two hundred reams at sixty percent. I'll take the water damage. I'll dry it in my building's stairwell, and I'll sell it to writers who cannot afford Jackson's price. In six months, I'll buy your good paper at your good price, and I'll pay on delivery, not on ninety days."

"And in six months, when you are a competitor?"

"Then I'll still be the only man who paid you on delivery."

Mrs. Chowdhury picked up her tea, drank it down as if it were medicine, and set the cup on the table.

"Seventy percent," she said. "And you carry it yourself. I will not pay for the truck."

That night John carried the first bale up five flights of stairs, and then another, and then another, until his hands were raw and his shirt was ruined and Mizan stood in the doorway of Room 113 with his mouth open, and Seema came down from the third floor, saw him, and asked, "What have you done?"

"I bought paper," John said.

"You bought rain," she said, and went to find a fan.

It took four days to dry the two hundred reams in the stairwell, and the whole building smelled of wet wood and lime, and the neighbor on the first floor complained, and Karim sent up tea twice a day without being asked.

But on the fifth day, a young poet named Anwar came to Room 113 with a manuscript and a problem: he had a hundred and fifty pages of poems and no money to have them printed. John sold him forty reams at cost, and Anwar wept, and John felt something turn over in his chest, something that was not pity and not pride but very close to both.

He wrote in the notebook that night: Paper $=$ power. I have the queen.

The Ledger of Ash and Iron

A weekly accounting, and a first taste of leverage.

✦

By the end of the first year John had a ledger, and the ledger had a name. He had bought it at a stationer's on Nilkhet Road, a thick green book with ruled columns, and he kept it in a locked biscuit tin under his bed because he had read enough crime novels to believe that ledgers should be locked up.

The ledger recorded three things: what he bought, what he sold, and what he owed. The first two columns grew. The third column was the interesting one, because it was not money. It was people.

There was Karim, who had lent him twelve thousand taka without interest and refused to take it back early, saying that a debt paid too quickly was a debt that had not been properly honored. There was Mrs. Chowdhury, whose two hundred damaged reams had sold out in nine days and who now called John on Wednesdays, always Wednesdays, to ask what he needed. There was Anwar the poet, whose little book of poems had sold four hundred copies and who came every month with a hundred taka and an apology for not having more.

And there was Seema, who had no entry in any column, and who appeared in the ledger anyway.

She appeared because she did his statistics. John could write a sentence that made a stranger cry, but he could not look at a stack of invoices and see a trend, and Seema could look at anything and see a trend. She came down from the third floor on Sunday evenings with a pencil and her father's briefcase and sat on the floor of Room 113 while he made tea, and she sorted his receipts into piles that she called "the past," "the lie," and "the future."

"The lie?" he asked the first time.

"Receipts from people who will never pay you," she said. "You keep them because they make you feel generous. They are not assets. They are stories."

She was right, of course. She was almost always right, and the strange thing was that it did not make her unbearable. It made the room feel safer than any room John had ever slept in.

On the last Sunday of that year, she finished the columns, closed the book, and said, "You are going to be rich."

"That is not a nice thing to say."

"It is not a nice fact." She looked at him. "It is just arithmetic."

"How rich?"

She told him the number. It was not a large number, but it was a number that did not exist anywhere in his family's history, and it sat in the room between them like a third person.

"I don't want to be rich," John said. "I want to write."

Seema raised an eyebrow. "Then why are you buying paper?"

"Because nobody at the press will give me what I want."

"What do you want?"

He did not have an answer ready, so he said the truest thing he had: "To keep the book on the pavement."

Seema looked at him for a moment too long, and then she stood up and put on her shoes, and said, "That is either the most romantic thing I have ever heard or the beginning of something terrible."

Years later, he would think that she had been exactly right, and that the only question was whether she had meant both halves at once.

Glass Walls and Paper Ghosts

An invitation to the new press, and the ghosts behind it.

✦

The new press had glass walls.

Jackson had moved, in the fourth year, from the room above the bakery on Elephant Road to the second floor of a building on Motijheel with a lobby that smelled of floor polish and a lift whose doors closed with a sound like a soft cough. He had not bought the building. He had bought the floor, or the lease of the floor, or something in between that John could not quite work out from the papers Jackson showed him, which were always, in some way, one paper short of a full answer.

"Look at the light," Jackson said, standing at the window with his hands behind his back. "A press should have light. Writers need to see what they are missing."

Below the glass was the intersection: three roads, a traffic light that did not work, a man selling coconuts from a cart, a hundred thousand people passing each other at speed. John stood beside the old man and watched the city come at the window and break against it.

"There are eleven authors on this floor," Jackson said. "Twelve, with you. Eleven ghosts and one living man."

"That's a strange way to describe your list."

"It's a strange list." Jackson turned. "Do you know why I published your book?"

"Because you read it on a train."

"Because I read it on a train and it was true." The old man came back to his desk, where the manuscript of someone else's novel sat in a neat stack, unread. "The eleven others write well. Two of them write better than you. But they write the books they think they should write. You wrote the book you could not help writing. That is the only kind of book that lasts."

"Then why do you keep the others?"

Jackson smiled, and for a moment John saw the whole machine of him: the rents, the paper, the percentages, the patient old arithmetic of staying alive in a business that killed everyone eventually.

"Because they pay for your books," Jackson said. "You are my loss leader, John. Do not look so wounded. It is a compliment. It means I am willing to lose money on you."

John looked at the glass wall, at the reflections of the two of them, and the city behind them, and thought: no. You are not losing money on me. You are losing money on the truth of me, and charging that loss to eleven other people, and calling it publishing.

But he said nothing. He was twenty-seven years old, and he had learned that the moment you say a thing out loud, you have to do something about it.

The Tuesday Ledger

A column changes, and a friendship is tested.

✦

The Tuesday Ledger was not an actual ledger. It was a meeting, held every Tuesday at the press, at which Jackson counted the money and the rest of them listened.

There were eleven authors, a production manager named Farid, an accountant named Miss Haq, and a woman named Renu who did the distribution from a desk by the door and who knew, John suspected, more about the business than all of them combined.

On the second Tuesday of the fifth year, John brought his green book and set it on the table.

"What is that?" Jackson asked.

"The truth," John said.

Anwar, who had been invited that day to discuss his second collection, looked at the book and then at the floor. Farid looked at the ceiling. Renu did not look away from John at all.

"Tell them," John said. "Tell them what the paper costs. Tell them what the press costs. Tell them what they are actually being paid per copy, and then tell them why every one of their books has left this room with a four-page catalogue in the back that none of us approved."

Jackson did not move for a long moment. Then he did the thing that John had not expected and would remember for the rest of his life: he opened the green book, and read it.

He read it slowly, page by page, for nine minutes, in a room so quiet the traffic outside seemed to have stopped. When he finished, he closed it and set it down and folded his hands.

"Mr. John has done his arithmetic," Jackson said. "I will not insult him by pretending it is wrong. The paper is dearer than it was. The press runs fewer books than it should. The catalogue pays for the rent on this glass floor, which is rented because eleven ghosts and one living man needed a place to be published." He turned to Anwar. "Your first collection sold four hundred copies. I printed six hundred. I have two hundred in the godown, and I will not sell them, because they are poetry, and poetry does not sell after the first month. I paid you your four hundred copies' worth. Did I not?"

Anwar nodded.

"And you were glad of it. As I was glad of you." The old man turned back to John. "There is no villain in this room. There is a business. If you want to save everyone, you must first own something. Do you own something, Mr. John?"

John looked at the green book.

"Not yet," he said.

"No," Jackson agreed. "Not yet."

The Open Ledger

An offer of partnership, and the price attached to it.

✦

Jackson's offer came in the spring, on paper so thick it felt like an apology.

Partnership. Twenty-five percent of the press, in exchange for the paper contracts and the distribution list. Effective the first of next month.

John read it three times in the lobby, standing up, while the lift doors opened and closed behind him. Twenty-five percent. A room with glass walls. His name, small, on the spine of every book that left the building.

He took it upstairs. Jackson was at his desk with his too-big watch and his pen between two fingers, and he did not pretend to be surprised.

"Why now?" John asked.

"Because you are going to leave," Jackson said. "You are going to take the paper and the authors and the pavement, and you will start your own press, and in three years you will be me. I would rather you were me inside this building, where I can still see the light."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I will wish you well, and I will not publish your next book, and you will discover that the book trade is a small country and I have been its postmaster for thirty years."

John sat down. The chair was the same chair he had sat in at twenty-three, when a voice on the phone at three in the morning had told him he was a writer.

"Who gets the other seventy-five?" he asked.

"Farid has five. Renu has ten. The rest is mine."

"And the catalogue in the back of every book?"

Jackson's pen turned once, slowly, between his fingers. "The catalogue stays."

"Then I can't sign it."

The old man nodded, as if John had confirmed a diagnosis he had suspected for years. "You understand that this is not a moral refusal. It is a strategic one. You are not better than me. You are just younger, and you have not yet learned what the rent costs."

"Then teach me," John said. "Sell me the press. Not a share. The whole thing. Name a price."

Jackson looked up. For a moment, something crossed his face that John would spend years trying to name. It was not anger. It was closer to relief.

"You cannot afford it," Jackson said.

"Not today," John said. "Ask me in three years."

The Echo of the Coast

Cox's Bazar, his mother's house, and a question in the sand.

✦

✦

The board met on the last Monday of March, in the glass room on the top floor, and there were nine of them at the table, including Shafiq and Farid and two men from the bank who had been invited because the bank had begun to have opinions.

"I know what I am running."

"Then explain the donation of eleven thousand books. Explain the paper sold at cost in the flood year. Explain the clause."

"Because I was wrong," he said.
