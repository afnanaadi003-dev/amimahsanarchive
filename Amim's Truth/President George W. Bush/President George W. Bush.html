Biography & Political Trajectory

George Walker Bush was born on July 6, 1946, in New Haven, Connecticut, the eldest son of George H. W. Bush and Barbara Pierce Bush. He grew up in Midland and Houston, Texas, attended Phillips Academy in Andover, and earned a history degree at Yale University before serving as a pilot in the Texas Air National Guard.

After earning an MBA from Harvard Business School in 1975, he returned to Texas to build an oil and gas business. In 1989 he led a group that purchased the Texas Rangers, serving as the team's managing general partner and becoming a familiar public figure across the state.

Bush defeated incumbent Ann Richards in 1994 to become the 46th Governor of Texas, winning re-election in 1998 with nearly 69 percent of the vote. His record of bipartisan education reform and welfare changes became the platform for his 2000 presidential campaign, which he won after a closely contested recount in Florida.

The Wartime Presidency

Eight months into his presidency, the September 11, 2001 attacks reshaped his administration. Bush launched the war in Afghanistan to dismantle al-Qaeda and the Taliban, created the Department of Homeland Security, and signed the USA PATRIOT Act. In 2003 he ordered the invasion of Iraq, a decision that defined—and divided—his legacy.

Domestically he signed the No Child Left Behind Act, expanded Medicare prescription drug coverage, and passed major tax relief packages. His second term was dominated by the Iraq surge, the failed push for immigration reform, the federal response to Hurricane Katrina, and the 2008 financial crisis, which led to the Troubled Asset Relief Program and government-backed rescues of banks and automakers.

Since leaving office, Bush has devoted himself to the George W. Bush Presidential Center, the Bush Institute, and painting portraits of veterans and immigrants—turning his post-presidency into a study of service, dignity, and reconciliation.

The Bush Doctrine

Compassionate Conservatism & the Freedom Agenda

Bush argued that conservatism should be judged by how it treats the weakest citizens: schools that teach every child, charities empowered to serve, and abroad a foreign policy that pairs military resolve with the promotion of democratic institutions. After September 11, that vision hardened into the Bush Doctrine—preemption, unilateral action when necessary, and an insistence that liberty is universal.

Compassion Through Local Institutions

Social problems are best addressed by families, congregations, and community groups rather than distant bureaucracies.

Security Before All Else

After 9/11, the protection of the homeland became the organizing principle of his presidency and the justification for difficult wartime choices.

The 9/11 Presidency: A Case Study

WHAT: On September 11, 2001, al-Qaeda hijackers killed nearly 3,000 people in New York, Virginia, and Pennsylvania. Bush was reading to schoolchildren in Florida when he learned of the attacks.

RESPONSE: Within weeks his administration launched Operation Enduring Freedom in Afghanistan, created the Department of Homeland Security, and reorganized intelligence and counterterrorism policy around the threat of transnational terrorism.

CONTESTED LEGACY: The subsequent invasion of Iraq in 2003, justified by intelligence about weapons of mass destruction that proved wrong, became the most controversial decision of his presidency and remains central to how historians judge his tenure.
