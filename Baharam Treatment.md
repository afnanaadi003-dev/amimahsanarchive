# BAHARAM

**A film by Amim Ahsan**
Feature · Political epic · English · ≈175 minutes
Twenty-seven chapters in four parts.

---

## Logline

A king's roadmaster is handed a sealed order to burn nine granaries across one night, and discovers that the only way to carry it faithfully is to disobey it completely.

---

## Synopsis

### Part One — The Order

The kingdom of Kesh is not losing a war. It is losing an argument about who will be left to pay for it. East of the river Amu, the horsemen of the Aq have taken the outer provinces one ford at a time; the court in Sarand answers each loss with a memorandum.

Baharam is the king's roadmaster — the man who rides ahead of the army and decides where the army sleeps. He knows the Nine Milestones by the shape of their stones and the wells by their taste, and he knows the lower valley because he was born in it, in Tangab, where his sister Durr keeps the granary that feeds nine thousand people.

In council, the vizier Farrukh lays out nine granaries and a treasury that cannot survive both the war and the winter. The council chooses fire, and the king — twenty-nine, exact, frightened of being the king who loses the valley — chooses Tangab first, because the roadmaster's sister keeps it. He wants a man who will feel it. He says so, and it sounds like statecraft.

That night the order is written in the scriptorium. The seal-keeper Mirza is required to enter in the register that the valley will be told the fire was the enemy's, and he asks the king, quietly, which account history should keep. The king answers honestly, which is worse. Then he presses his ring into the wax.

Baharam is given four hours, a tired mare named Sabr, and the rule that he must not break the seal. On his way out of the city, Shirin — the king's sister, who keeps the ledger of what the palace spends and what it says it spent — gives him nothing but a look. She has already written a list of ten people who could have stopped the order, and only one of them is still worth hoping for.

### Part Two — The Road

The order travels at the speed of a tired horse. At the second milestone a relay keeper has been told to have a fresh mount ready and to ask no questions, and asks one anyway. At the camp, the frontier general Vahid has already guessed what is in the tube — because he has been living with the arithmetic for a year. He does not stop Baharam. He lets him through, so that the man who breaks the seal will be somebody he can name.

In the camp's pay tent, Baharam learns what the fire actually is: not a war measure but a collection. The grain is the army's arrears; the burned land forfeits to the crown for eleven years and can be leased for silver. Eight valleys become a line item, and no one has to say the word theft out loud.

At the bridge of seven arches he meets its builder, Anahita, seventy-one, who shows him the sluice boards under the fifth arch and explains that the Amu can be made to take the ford below at the width of the whole bridge. Under the arches live two hundred and eleven people — Bibi Sarvar's street of laundry, bread ovens and goats, where the rent is nothing because the roof is a bridge. Farid, twenty-three and commanding, has read Baharam's survey and used it on the march; he is the only man in the film who is still winning.

In the rain, an envoy of Temur of the Aq offers the bridge and the valley's survival in exchange for the one thing that cannot be carried away. Before dawn a palace courier catches Baharam with a second letter: acknowledge the order, or come back and explain yourself. Baharam tells the boy to report one seal, unbroken, and lets him go.

### Part Three — The Sealed Word

At Tangab, before dawn, Baharam walks into his sister's granary and opens the order in front of her and the village. Durr reads the clause that names her own roof and does not weep; she asks him what he intends, and then she starts moving people up the ridge road because there is work to be done and grief is not work.

At first light a relay boy brings word that the other eight granaries are burning — eight columns of smoke standing straight up in still air, eight roadmasters who obeyed. Tangab is one clean patch of sky. Durr does the arithmetic out loud: one granary now has to feed eight valleys, and every morning they will have to decide whether to open the doors.

At the bridge, the two hundred and eleven are moved up onto the roadway — a quarter of an hour they have, by Bibi Sarvar's count, and Baharam carries an old man up the stair himself. Then the boards go up under the fifth arch, four men and an hour, and the water comes down the ford like a hand across a table. Three hundred horse of the Aq are in the crossing. The bridge holds. The ford is gone. The valley is saved by drowning it, and the ledger of what it cost begins that afternoon.

### Part Four — The Answer

In Sarand the king does not thank him. He confesses instead: he wrote an order a good man would refuse, because he could not afford the valley and could not bear to be the king who said so. Baharam tells him he used nine thousand people as a question. The king agrees, and offers him the twelfth chair — the reader of orders, a new office whose only power is that every order must be read aloud, exactly as written, before it is voted on.

Shirin keeps the accounting. Mirza must enter a cause of death in the register that he has no word for, and writes, in a hand much smaller than his usual: BY ORDER. A widow refuses the schedule for her husband and asks only that his name be written down. The valley's assessment is cut to a tenth by a general who laughs, once.

Baharam goes back to the valley and takes a farmer's fist without putting a hand up. Vahid stands with him at the new channel and admits he is tired of being forgiven. At the bridge, Anahita builds a new oven on the roadway because she promised a six-year-old, and Temur's envoy comes under a white rag to ask for his dead and to say, badly, that his master is glad the bridge is standing and does not know why.

On the road out of Tangab, Baharam ties a red rag to the first milestone so the next man knows he is not the first one there. A boy on a mule asks him what a roadmaster does. He says: he goes first, and then he comes back and tells them what he saw, truthfully, even if it costs him the horse. And if nobody believes him?

Then he goes again.

## Characters

- **Baharam** — roadmaster of the east road, thirty-eight, three fingers on his left hand. Not a rebel. A man with a professional objection to building anything on top of people.
- **King Zav** — twenty-nine, exact, and afraid of being the king who lost the valley. Signs things and looks away.
- **Shirin** — his sister. Keeps the true ledger. Writes lists of people who could have stopped things, so that next time she knows where to start.
- **Durr** — granary-keeper of Tangab, Baharam's sister. Has counted the grain twice and can tell you exactly how many days the valley can eat.
- **Sardar Vahid** — holds the east bank with eleven hundred men and one bridge. Would rather be told the truth than spared it.
- **Anahita** — the engineer. Built the seven arches forty years ago. Has been waiting for someone to ask her the right question.
- **Vizier Farrukh** — believes a kingdom is a balance sheet with a flag.
- **Farid** — twenty-three, officer commanding the bridge. Read Baharam's survey and used it. The only man still winning.
- **Mirza** — the seal-keeper. Thirty-one years of entries; one word he has never had to write.
- **Bibi Sarvar** — counts the people, not the arches.
- **Durr's village** — Hassan, the well-keeper's sister, the arch families: the two hundred and eleven, and the nine thousand behind them.
- **Temur of the Aq** — never on screen with a face until an envoy speaks for him. Wants the bridge, because a bridge is a promise.

## Themes

1. **Procedure as morality.** The film's decisive act is not violence but a reading: an order opened in public, aloud, in front of the people it costs.
2. **The arithmetic of the powerful.** Every cruelty in the film arrives as a number that is correct.
3. **Water and fire.** Fire is the instrument of the state; water is the instrument of the valley. Both destroy. Only one leaves silt to plant in.
4. **Coming back.** The roadmaster's real job is the second half: returning and telling the truth about what he saw.

---

## Visual and production approach

- **One night, one day, one month.** Parts One to Three run through a single night into a single morning; Part Four spans ten days and then a month later.
- **Palette.** Lamp and wax in the palace; wet slate and road dust outside; brown flood and white sky at the bridge; silt grey for the whole last part.
- **No aerial shots of the flood.** The audience stays at eye level with the people deciding, and with the two hundred and eleven on the roadway.
- **Screenplay format.** Each chapter opens with a slate naming camera, stock and ISO, costume, and environment, so the chapter can be shot as a unit.
- **Runtime.** Twenty-seven chapters, eighty-four scenes, ≈20,200 words of script, ≈175 minutes.
