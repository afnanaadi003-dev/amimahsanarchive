Before I Die Wore

by Amim Ahsan · writing under the pen name Amimul Ahsan

Every mask has an ending.

Julian Mercer has six to eight weeks to live, a marriage built on careful silences, and a father whose quiet sacrifice he has spent four years misunderstanding. On the afternoon the diagnosis lands, he walks out of a Madison Avenue law firm and begins a list of thirty-four unfinished truths. What follows is not a bucket list but a reckoning — a burning of masks from a Manhattan boardroom to a frozen pier in Harbor Cove, Maine, where the last thing waiting for him is the truth about the life he thought he had built.

Act I · Chapter OneBefore I Die Wore

The Diagnosis & The Reckoning

November. A room with a humming light.

✦

The fluorescent bulb above Dr. Aris Thorne’s desk had a faint, high-pitched hum—the exact frequency of a mosquito trapped behind glass. For three years, Julian had sat in this same leather chair every six months, nodding through routine blood panels and cholesterol lectures while checking emails under the edge of the mahogany table.

Today, his phone lay face down on his knee, silent.

Dr. Thorne didn’t look at his monitor. He took off his reading glasses, folded them carefully, and set them on top of a manila folder that seemed far too thin to hold the end of a life.

“Julian,” the doctor said, his voice stripped of its usual brisk warmth. “I wish there were a softer way to lay this out, or a second trial we hadn’t already exhausted in the preliminary pathology. The biopsy results came back from Boston this morning.”

Julian stared at a framed print of a sailboat on the wall behind the doctor’s shoulder. The water in the painting was an impossible, flat cerulean. “How long?”

“Without intervention, six to eight weeks. With aggressive palliative radiation, perhaps four months. Five, if your body responds unusually well.”

Four months. Julian did the math automatically, the way he did quarterly projections at the firm. Four months meant he would miss the autumn merger. It meant he had paid for an annual gym membership three days ago that he would never use. It meant the half-painted guest bedroom at home would stay taped off with blue painter’s strip until someone else had to peel it down.

“Is there a mistake?” Julian asked. His voice sounded remarkably steady, almost bored, as if he were disputing a billing error on a hotel receipt. “A lab mix-up? Different Julian Mercer?”

“I had them run the tissue markers twice before I called you in,” Dr. Thorne said gently. “I’m sorry, Julian. Truly.”

The rest of the appointment dissolved into muffled syllables—referrals, oncology coordinators, pain management protocols, pamphlets slid across the blotter in crisp white envelopes. Julian took them all. He shook the doctor’s hand. His palm was dry and cool. He thanked the receptionist on his way out, apologizing automatically when he brushed against a potted fern near the exit.

Then the heavy glass doors of the medical plaza swung shut behind him, and the November air hit his face.

For a long moment, Julian couldn’t move.

Everything outside looked wrong. Not distorted, just unbearably sharp, as if someone had turned the contrast up on the entire city until the edges of things hurt to look at. Across the street, a young woman in a yellow coat was laughing into her phone, shifting her weight from one heel to the other while waiting for the crosswalk signal. A stray pigeon pecked furiously at a dropped sesame bun near the curb. Above the brick facade of the pharmacy, a single copper-colored leaf clung to a sycamore branch, trembling in a breeze Julian couldn’t even feel.

He had walked down Fourth Street four hundred times. He knew where the sidewalk cracked near the dry cleaner’s; he knew the exact timing of the traffic lights between the clinic and the subway station. Yet as he stepped forward, his shoes felt unfamiliar against the concrete.

A delivery courier brushed past his shoulder, muttering a hurried “Excuse me,” eyes locked on a route map. Julian watched him disappear into the crowd, struck by a sudden, dizzying realization: That man thinks he has forever. Every person hurrying past him—the woman in the yellow coat, the barista wiping down the window of the corner café, the businessman arguing about a delayed shipment—was moving through the afternoon as though time were an infinite, renewable currency.

Julian reached into his coat pocket and touched the stiff edge of the white pamphlets.

His phone buzzed against his thigh. He pulled it out and looked at the screen. It was a calendar reminder: 3:30 PM – Q4 Budget Alignment w/ Marcus & Sarah.

Julian looked from the glowing screen to the trembling copper leaf on the sycamore tree, and then down the long, crowded stretch of Fourth Street. For the first time in fifteen years, he didn’t reply, didn’t dismiss the alert, and didn’t walk toward the subway. He stood still in the middle of the sidewalk, letting the tide of strangers part around him, listening to the sound of his own breathing.

The clock had started. And for the first time, he was actually awake to hear it tick.

Chapter TwoBefore I Die Wore

The Quiet Routine

The kitchen, the knife, the ordinary Tuesday.

✦

By the time Julian’s key turned in the deadbolt, the November dusk had settled deep into the brownstone hallway, turning the familiar brass coat rack and umbrella stand into soft, dark silhouettes.

Before stepping inside, he stood on the welcome mat for ten seconds, rehearsing his face. He smoothed the tension out of his forehead, tucked the white envelopes from Dr. Thorne deep into the inside lining of his wool coat, and hung the coat on the far hook behind his heavier winter parka.

“Jules? Is that you?”

Clara’s voice drifted out from the kitchen, accompanied by the rhythmic, percussive thwack-thwack-thwack of a chef’s knife hitting a wooden cutting board.

“Yeah,” Julian called back, amazed at how easily the word came out—warm, slightly tired, completely ordinary. “Sorry I’m late. Traffic near the river was a mess.”

He walked down the narrow hall and paused in the kitchen doorway. For years, this room had been merely a staging ground between work and sleep—a place where he swallowed cold coffee while reading briefs or complained about the dishwasher’s rattling drain pump. Tonight, it felt like a painting he had never bothered to step close enough to examine.

Steam curled lazily from a copper pot on the back burner, fogging the lower pane of the window overlooking the garden. Clara stood at the island with her hair pinned up in a loose, lopsided knot, a smudge of flour or garlic skin near her wrist. She was chopping scallions with quick, practiced flicks of her fingers, humming half a bar of a song she never quite finished—a habit that, only yesterday, Julian had barely registered.

“How was the checkup?” she asked without looking up, scraping the green rings into a ceramic bowl. “Did Thorne finally scold you about the espresso?”

“Told me to cut back to two cups,” Julian lied smoothly, walking over to kiss the top of her head. Her hair smelled faintly of cedar shampoo and onions. “And to stop skipping lunch on Thursdays.”

“I’ve been telling you that for six years.” She glanced up, smiling, and paused with the knife resting on the board. “You look pale, though. Rough afternoon with Marcus?”

Julian felt a sudden, sharp ache behind his ribs—not physical pain, just the sheer weight of the wall he had just built between them. One sentence would shatter the steam-fogged window, stop the knife, and replace that easy smile with a grief she would carry for the rest of her life. He wasn’t ready to hand her that weight yet. He wanted to give her one more normal Tuesday.

“Just a long day,” he said, reaching out to tuck a stray strand of hair behind her ear, letting his fingertips linger against her cheek a fraction of a second longer than usual. “Go sit down for ten minutes. I’ll finish the prep.”

“Who are you and what have you done with my husband?” she teased, though she untied her apron and handed it over.

After dinner, the quiet routine unfolded with an almost unbearable tenderness. Julian watched Clara curl into the corner of the sofa with her legs tucked under a knit throw, her thumb sliding down the spine of a paperback to hold the pages flat. He noticed the faint silver scar near her left eyebrow from a childhood bicycle fall; he noticed the way the lamplight caught the fine, almost invisible gold hairs along her forearm; he noticed how the old radiator by the bookshelf hissed twice before settling into a steady, comforting click.

Normally, Julian would have had his laptop open on his knees by 8:30, answering emails until midnight. Tonight, he left his briefcase untouched by the front door. He sat beside her, resting one hand on her ankle beneath the blanket, memorizing the steady, unhurried rise and fall of her breathing.

When Clara finally yawned, kissed his shoulder, and went upstairs to brush her teeth, Julian stayed on the sofa in the dim light of the single floor lamp. The house settled around him—the distant hum of the refrigerator, the faint creak of floorboards overhead as Clara moved from the bathroom to the bedroom, the muffled bark of a neighbor’s dog two yards over.

He reached into his pocket, pulled out his phone, and opened a blank note.

For thirty-eight years, he had treated his life like a rough draft—something he would properly live once the next promotion cleared, once the mortgage was paid down, once the guest room was finished. Looking around the quiet living room, at Clara’s half-empty mug of chamomile tea resting on a coaster, Julian realized he had spent decades rushing past the very things he was now terrified to lose.

Beneath the glowing cursor on his screen, he typed three words at the top of the page: Before I Die.

Chapter ThreeBefore I Die Wore

The Unwritten List

2:14 a.m., a linen notebook, thirty-four lines.

✦

At 2:14 a.m., the bedroom was pitch-dark save for the faint amber glow of the streetlamp filtering through the Venetian blinds. Beside Julian, Clara slept on her side, one hand tucked beneath her cheek, her breathing slow and rhythmic.

Julian lay flat on his back, staring at the faint hairline crack in the plaster ceiling. Every time he closed his eyes, Dr. Thorne’s voice echoed back in the dark: Six to eight weeks. Four months, if you’re lucky.

If he stayed in bed, he would spend the next four hours calculating dates, drafting imaginary wills in his head, and listening to the grandfather clock downstairs tick away seconds he couldn’t afford to lose.

Carefully, sliding his legs out from under the duvet so the mattress barely shifted, Julian slipped into his robe and padded barefoot down the stairs to his study.

He didn’t turn on the overhead light. Instead, he clicked on the small brass banker’s lamp on his desk, casting a tight pool of warm green-tinted light across the blotter. Pushing aside a stack of quarterly merger contracts, he opened the bottom drawer and pulled out a heavy, linen-bound notebook he had bought three years ago in Florence and never once opened—saving it, he had told himself at the time, for something “worthy.”

Now, he uncapped his fountain pen.

At first, the nib hovered over the cream-colored page, trembling slightly. What do you write when you are editing thirty-eight years down to a final draft?

He started with the obvious things—the cowardly, small-scale regrets that had accumulated like dust in the corners of his life.

1.Quit the firm. Tomorrow. Not a two-week notice—walk out.
2.Finish painting the guest room so Clara doesn’t have to look at blue tape when I’m gone.
3.Call Daniel.

Julian paused at number three, his pen leaving a small dark inkblot on the paper. He hadn’t spoken to his younger brother since their father’s funeral four years ago—an argument over estate paperwork that had hardened into pride, and then into silence. Four years had felt like a temporary pause when Julian assumed he had forty more to fix it. Now, four years was nearly his entire remaining lifespan.

The realization broke something open inside him. The pen began to move faster, scratching across the heavy paper in the quiet room.

It wasn’t a tourist’s bucket list. There were no skydives, no pyramids, no Michelin-starred tasting menus. It was a ledger of reckoning—every cowardly silence, every half-built bridge, every truth he had swallowed to keep the peace or protect his ego.

7.Burn the unopened letters in the cedar chest.
11.Tell Marcus what actually happened with the Henderson account.
14.Take Clara back to the coast in Maine, out of season, when the wind is cold.
19.Find out who kept sending Dad those bank transfers in 2018.
23.Stop apologizing for taking up space.

By the time the first pale gray light of dawn began to bleed around the edges of the study curtains, Julian had filled three pages. He looked down at the numbered lines, counting them twice.

Thirty-four items. One for every remaining piece of unfinished business he refused to carry into the dark.

Julian closed the linen notebook, resting his palm flat against the cover. Upstairs, the floorboards creaked as Clara stirred in her sleep. For the first time since walking out of Dr. Thorne’s office, the hollow terror in Julian’s chest had shifted into something sharper, heavier, and undeniably alive: purpose.

Chapter FourBefore I Die Wore

Burning the Mask

The twenty-fourth floor. One word: no.

✦

The glass conference room on the twenty-fourth floor smelled of stale espresso and expensive cologne. Around the polished walnut table, six senior partners of Vance & Sterling sat in identical navy suits, staring at a projected spreadsheet that tracked Q4 client retention down to the decimal.

Julian sat at the far end, his hands folded calmly over a single, blank sheet of company letterhead.

At the head of the table, Marcus Vance tapped a silver Montblanc pen against his legal pad. “The bottom line is simple,” Marcus said, his voice carrying the practiced, jovial authority that had intimidated junior associates for twenty years. “Henderson Logistics wants a twelve percent fee reduction before signing the renewal. We absorb the hit on the junior billing hours, push the associate team through December weekends, and keep the account on our books through Q1. Julian, you’re lead on Henderson—draft the revised retainer by five.”

Around the table, heads nodded automatically. Sarah, sitting to Julian’s left, offered him a tight, sympathetic grimace before returning to her tablet. For nine years, this was the script: Marcus demanded the impossible, Julian smoothed the edges, swallowed the resentment, and worked until midnight to make the numbers look effortless.

Julian looked at the numbers glowing on the wall. Then he looked out the floor-to-ceiling window behind Marcus, where an autumn storm was rolling in over the harbor, turning the water slate-gray.

“No,” Julian said.

The tapping of Marcus’s pen stopped. Across the table, a junior analyst froze mid-sip of his water.

Marcus blinked, chuckling softly as if he had misheard a punchline. “Excuse me?”

“I’m not drafting the retainer,” Julian repeated. His voice wasn’t raised; there was no tremor of anger in it, just the flat, startling clarity of someone who had stopped caring about the outcome of the room. “And we shouldn’t keep Henderson. Their director of operations has lied about their compliance audits twice this year, and we’ve been billing eighty-hour weeks just to bury their liability.”

Silence dropped over the twenty-fourth floor like a heavy curtain. Sarah stared at her hands, her face draining of color.

Marcus’s smile vanished, replaced by a slow, dangerous flush creeping up his collar. “Julian, if you’re having a rough week, take the afternoon. Don’t come into a partner briefing and undermine—”

“I’m not having a rough week, Marcus,” Julian interrupted gently. He picked up the Montblanc pen lying beside his notepad, unscrewed the cap, and wrote three sentences across the company letterhead. “I’m having the only week that matters.”

He slid the paper across the smooth walnut surface until it stopped against Marcus’s elbow.

“Item one on my list,” Julian said, standing up and buttoning his jacket. “Sarah, you should take the senior director chair on the Sterling account—you’ve been doing the work for three years while Marcus took the credit at the board dinners.”

Sarah’s mouth opened slightly, no sound coming out.

“And Marcus,” Julian added, resting his knuckles briefly on the back of his chair, “tell Arthur Henderson to hire an actual compliance officer before he ends up in federal court. Have a good quarter.”

Julian didn’t wait for the shouting to start behind him. He walked out of the glass doors, past the stunned stares of the reception desk, and straight into his office. He left his laptop open on the desk, left his framed commendations on the wall, and took only two things: his worn leather coat from the hook and the small brass compass his father had given him twenty years ago.

As the express elevator dropped twenty-four floors in twelve seconds, Julian caught his reflection in the brushed steel doors. For the first time in a decade, the man looking back didn’t look like a stranger wearing someone else’s face.

When he stepped out onto the plaza into the first cold drops of November rain, his phone was already vibrating violently in his pocket—Marcus, then Sarah, then the firm’s HR director.

Julian smiled, pressed the power button until the screen went completely black, and dropped the phone into his coat pocket. One mask burned. Thirty-three items left.

Chapter FiveBefore I Die Wore

The First Ghost

Cedar Avenue. Four years of silence, and a thermos of burned coffee.

✦

The hardware store on Cedar Avenue smelled of cut pine, machine oil, and damp sawdust—the exact scent that had clung to Daniel’s flannel jackets since they were teenagers.

Julian stood outside the plate-glass window for nearly ten minutes before pushing the door open, the small brass bell overhead chiming brightly in the quiet afternoon. Behind the counter, Daniel was bent over a clipboard, his dark hair threaded with more gray at the temples than Julian remembered, his shoulders broader from years of lifting lumber instead of spreadsheets.

Four years. Four years since the afternoon outside the probate lawyer’s office when Julian had accused Daniel of draining their father’s savings during his final months, and Daniel had walked away without saying a word in his own defense.

Julian stepped up to the counter, resting his hands flat on the scarred wood. “Hey, Danny.”

Daniel’s pen froze mid-signature. Slowly, he lifted his head. For a fraction of a second, his eyes widened with the reflex of old affection—before the familiar wall slid smoothly back into place.

“Julian,” Daniel said evenly, setting the pen down. “You’re a long way from downtown. Need drywall anchors, or did you come to audit my register?”

Julian winced, taking the hit without flinching. He deserved it. “I came to apologize. And to talk, if you’ll give me ten minutes.”

Daniel studied him closely—taking in the absence of a briefcase, the uncharacteristically rumpled coat, the shadow beneath Julian’s eyes that hadn’t been there four years ago. After a long silence, Daniel reached under the counter, flipped a laminated Back in 15 Minutes sign against the front glass, and nodded toward the back loading bay where two folding chairs sat beside a stack of cedar planks.

They sat with a chipped thermos between them. Neither spoke until the rain began drumming steadily against the corrugated tin roof.

“I was wrong at the lawyer’s office,” Julian said, staring down at his hands. “About the money. About you taking advantage of Dad when he was sick. I looked at the numbers, assumed the worst, and never once asked you what happened.”

Daniel let out a short, humorless breath, leaning back against the wood stack. “You didn’t ask because you already had the verdict written, Jules. You always did. You showed up for two hours every second Sunday, wrote a check, and acted like you were carrying the whole family on your back.”

“I know,” Julian said quietly. “I was angry that he was dying, and it was easier to be angry at you.”

Daniel looked away toward the open bay door, watching the rain puddle on the concrete alley. His jaw worked silently for a moment before the anger seemed to drain out of his shoulders, leaving behind an old, bone-deep exhaustion.

“I didn’t spend a dime of Dad’s money on myself, Julian,” Daniel said softly. “The forty thousand missing from his checking account? Dad made me wire it to an escrow account in Chicago three months before he died. Made me swear on Mom’s grave I wouldn’t tell you.”

Julian frowned, turning toward his brother. “Chicago? Why?”

“Because of your first firm,” Daniel said, meeting his eyes finally. “When your old partner embezzled client funds in 2018 and left you holding half the liability, you told Dad you were going to lose your house. You thought the bank mysteriously restructured your debt out of the goodness of their hearts?”

The air seemed to rush out of Julian’s lungs. He remembered that winter—the sleepless nights, the sudden, inexplicable anonymous settlement that had cleared his name and saved his license right before he married Clara. He had spent six years believing it was a legal technicality negotiated by his attorneys.

“Dad liquidated his pension and cashed out his savings to cover your shortfall,” Daniel continued quietly. “He knew your pride would never let you accept charity from him, so he routed it through a blind trust in Chicago and told me to take the blame if you ever noticed the missing balance.”

Julian sat frozen on the folding chair, the sound of the rain suddenly roaring in his ears. For four years, he had carried a righteous, bitter grudge like a badge of honor—condemning his brother for the very sacrifice that had built Julian’s entire career.

“Why didn’t you tell me at the funeral?” Julian whispered, his throat tight.

“Because a promise to a dying man still meant something to me,” Daniel said simply. Then his gaze softened, looking more closely at Julian’s face. “Why are you really here today, Jules? You didn’t drive across town in the middle of a Tuesday just to rehash 2018.”

Julian looked at his brother—really looked at him—and felt the terrible temptation to spill everything right there among the cedar planks and rain. Instead, he swallowed hard and forced a crooked, fragile smile.

“Because four years is too long to stay angry at the only person who remembers what Mom’s voice sounded like,” Julian said. “And because I realized I don’t have time left to waste on being right.”

Daniel studied him for a beat longer, then reached over, unscrewed the cap of the chipped thermos, and poured dark coffee into the plastic cup, holding it out.

“It’s terrible coffee,” Daniel warned gruffly. “Burned three hours ago.”

Julian took the cup, wrapping both cold hands around the warmth. “Best thing I’ve had all week.”

Chapter SixBefore I Die Wore

Things Left Unsaid

Sunday dinner, the good porcelain, and breadcrumbs in the dark.

✦

Sunday dinner at Evelyn Mercer’s dining table operated like a well-rehearsed stage play where everyone knew their lines except the person who had rewritten the ending.

The long oak table was set with the good porcelain—the blue-rimmed plates Evelyn only brought out when both her sons were under the same roof. Roasted rosemary chicken sat at the center beside bowls of mashed potatoes and buttered green beans, steaming gently beneath the brass chandelier.

When Julian and Clara walked in together, Evelyn’s eyes flicked past Julian to Daniel, who had arrived ten minutes earlier and was standing awkwardly near the sideboard holding a glass of sparkling water. For a second, the air in the dining room went completely still. Evelyn’s hand trembled slightly against the back of her chair before she pressed her lips together, hiding the shock behind brisk maternal authority.

“Well,” Evelyn said, smoothing her linen napkin over her lap. “Don’t stand in the doorway letting the draft in. Sit down before the gravy skin forms.”

That was all it took. Four years of silence between her sons was folded neatly under the tablecloth without a single direct question, replaced instantly by the safer, familiar currency of small talk.

“Clara, darling, tell me how the gallery restoration is going,” Evelyn began, passing the bread basket clockwise. “And Julian, Marcus called my landline yesterday afternoon—said something about you taking a sabbatical? I told him he must have misheard.”

Clara paused mid-reach for the butter knife, glancing sharply at Julian. “Marcus called your mother?”

Julian took a slow sip of water, feeling the heat of three pairs of eyes settling on him. “I resigned on Tuesday,” he said evenly. “Cleaned out my desk.”

The clink of silverware stopped.

Evelyn stared at him as though he had just announced he was moving to Mars. “Resigned? Julian, you’re six months away from senior equity partnership. You don’t just walk away from nine years of billable hours without a lateral offer lined up.”

“I didn’t want a lateral offer, Mom,” Julian said gently. “I realized I’ve spent nine years building someone else’s balance sheet while missing everything that actually belongs to me.”

“That sounds lovely on a greeting card,” Evelyn replied, her voice tightening with the anxious pragmatism that had carried her through widowhood. “It doesn’t pay property taxes in Westchester. Next spring, when the market corrects, you’re going to wish you hadn’t acted on impulse.”

Next spring.

Julian looked down at his plate, then up at the faces gathered around the oak table. Across from him, Daniel was watching him closely—not with their mother’s annoyance, but with the quiet, unsettled suspicion that had lingered since Tuesday afternoon in the lumber bay. Beside Julian, Clara’s fingers had gone still against the stem of her wine glass, her smile fading into furrowed concern.

“I made sure the mortgage is covered through next year,” Julian said softly, looking directly at Clara, trying to press a message into her eyes without speaking the words aloud. “And I updated the beneficiary paperwork on the life policy and the accounts on Thursday. Everything’s in Clara’s name alone now—no probate, no joint delays.”

The table went quiet again, heavier this time.

Clara set her wine glass down with a sharp click. “Julian, why are you talking like we’re preparing for a hurricane? First you quit your job without telling me, and now you’re rewriting estate paperwork on a Thursday afternoon?”

“Because people wait until it’s too late to make things simple for the people they love,” Julian replied, forcing warmth into his voice even as his chest tightened. He turned toward Daniel, offering a small, deliberate nod. “And because I want Danny to have Dad’s workshop tools and the cedar chest from the attic. They’ve been sitting in boxes at my place collecting dust when they belong with someone who actually builds things.”

“Jules,” Daniel said quietly, leaning forward over his untouched plate. “Stop.”

Evelyn waved a dismissive hand, eager to steer the conversation back toward solid ground. “Oh, there’s plenty of time to sort through your father’s attic next summer when we repaint the garage. We don’t need to divide up the house over Sunday roast. Now eat—your potatoes are getting cold.”

Julian looked around the table—at his mother already planning a summer painting project eight months away, at Clara mentally mapping out gallery schedules for the spring, at the effortless, unshakeable faith they all held that tomorrow was guaranteed simply because today had arrived.

He picked up his fork and smiled, swallowing the truth that sat like lead at the base of his throat. He had tried to leave breadcrumbs in the dark, hoping someone might trace the path before the storm hit. Instead, he realized how blinding normal life truly was: when people expect you to grow old beside them, they cannot hear a goodbye even when you speak it right across the table.

Chapter SevenBefore I Die Wore

The Hidden Box

A strongbox under the cedar chest, and four words in block print.

✦

The cedar chest in the attic smelled faintly of mothballs and dried lavender, its brass hinges groaning when Julian pried the lid open Tuesday morning while Clara was at the gallery.

Item seven on his notebook list sat waiting near the bottom: Burn the unopened letters in the cedar chest.

For years, Julian had kept the heavy wooden box pushed behind suitcases and holiday ornaments, telling himself he would sort through his father’s personal effects when he wasn’t buried in Q4 filings. Now, kneeling on the unfinished pine floorboards with a dusty cardboard box beside him for donations, he lifted out stacks of yellowed tax returns, faded warranty booklets, and bound bundles of correspondence tied with kitchen twine.

Beneath a thick wool army blanket at the very bottom of the chest, Julian’s fingers brushed against something cold and metallic—not paper, but a small, locked steel strongbox wedged tight against the cedar siding.

He frowned, pulling it into the pale shaft of light falling from the gable window. There was no key taped to the lid, and the small tumbler lock was rusted solid from years of attic humidity. Downstairs, Julian retrieved a flathead screwdriver and a hammer from the utility closet, returned to the attic, and with two sharp taps, popped the corroded latch open.

Inside lay three items: an old Polaroid photograph curled at the edges, a stamped passport belonging to his father, and a bound ledger of wire receipts dated between 2016 and 2019.

Julian picked up the Polaroid first. In it, his father—younger, wearing a canvas fishing jacket—stood beside a stone pier in a coastal town Julian didn’t recognize. Beside him stood a woman with dark hair and a laughing child no older than six or seven. Written on the white bottom margin in his father’s neat, unmistakable block print were four words: Harbor Cove, August 2017.

Julian’s brow furrowed. In August 2017, his father had told both Julian and Daniel he was spending two weeks at a cardiac recovery clinic in Upstate New York.

Heart beating a little faster, Julian opened the ledger of wire receipts. Every month, from January 2016 until two months before his father’s death in 2020, an automated transfer of two thousand dollars had been sent from a secondary account at First Merchants Bank to an account under the name M. Vance Holdings LLC—the exact parent holding company owned by Marcus Vance, Julian’s former senior partner.

Julian stared at the printed routing numbers until the ink blurred.

His father had never worked in finance. He had spent thirty-five years as a civil engineer for the state highway department; he didn’t even know Marcus Vance existed—or so Julian had believed. Yet here, stamped in crisp bank ink, were nearly four years of recurring wire transfers flowing directly from his father’s private account into Marcus’s holding company, ending the exact month Julian had been promoted to lead the Henderson account.

Julian sat back on his heels in the quiet dust of the attic, the Polaroid trembling slightly between his thumb and forefinger.

The math didn’t work. His father hadn’t just quietly paid off Julian’s 2018 debt through a blind trust in Chicago—he had been paying Marcus Vance directly for years. And standing beside his father in that 2017 photograph, smiling into the sun on an unfamiliar pier, was a woman whose face Julian had never seen in any family album.

Somewhere downstairs, the front door clicked shut—Clara returning early from the gallery. Julian quickly gathered the strongbox, the ledger, and the Polaroid, sliding them beneath the floorboards behind the cedar chest just as footsteps sounded on the hallway stairs. For the first time since Dr. Thorne’s diagnosis, the clock ticking inside Julian’s chest wasn’t filled with resignation—it was sharp with sudden, cold suspicion.

Chapter EightBefore I Die Wore

A Stranger’s Perspective

The northbound train, and a man who had already survived his own countdown.

✦

The commuter train heading north along the Hudson River was half-empty at two in the afternoon, swaying gently over the tracks as gray November water slid past the rain-streaked windows.

Julian sat alone in a four-seat bay near the rear of the car, the folded Polaroid from the attic resting inside his coat pocket like a hot coal. Across the aisle, an elderly man in a worn tweed overcoat sat nursing a paper cup of tea, watching the river with quiet, unhurried attention.

Every few minutes, Julian’s fingers drifted unconsciously to his chest—checking the dull, persistent ache beneath his sternum, testing how much air his lungs could draw before the tightness caught him. “You can’t drive down to Boston like this,” Nadia said quietly, her expression stripped of all anger and defiance, leaving only a profound, heavy sorrow. “You’re trying to outrun a clock that doesn’t care how many miles you put between yourself and New York.”

Julian looked out the frosted windshield at the gray Maine woods stretching endlessly into the dusk. The romantic notions of burning masks, riding trains, and playing the stoic detective on a righteous crusade felt suddenly pathetic against the stark, brutal reality of his failing neurology. He was dying. Not in some distant, poetic third-act climax, but right here, in the front seat of a borrowed car on a forgotten stretch of highway, with his fingers going numb.

“I have to get to Boston, Nadia,” Julian whispered, turning his head slowly to meet her eyes. “Clara is at that clinic. And if I don’t get there tonight, I may never get the chance to tell her why I was wrong about everything.”

Chapter SeventeenBefore I Die Wore

A Night Without Tomorrow

A Boston motel, two cups of coffee, and the truth about ordinary terror.

✦

The Boston motel room smelled faintly of bleach and damp radiator iron, its single window looking out over an asphalt parking lot slick with freezing November drizzle.

Julian sat on the edge of the double mattress, the room illuminated only by the amber sodium glare of a streetlamp leaking through the cracked blinds. His right hand still buzzed with a faint, residual tremor, but the corticosteroid Nadia had forced him to swallow outside Portland had beaten the white static back into a manageable, dull hum behind his temples.

Beside him on the nightstand lay his phone—power back on, screen dark. He hadn’t called Clara yet. He hadn’t called Daniel, or Marcus, or the State Bar ethics committee.

The door clicked open, and Nadia stepped inside, shaking the cold rain from her canvas jacket and setting a brown paper bag of lukewarm takeout and two paper cups of black coffee on the small laminate desk.

“Traffic on the interstate was crawling past Portsmouth,” Nadia said quietly, tossing her keys onto the dresser. She glanced over at Julian, her eyes searching his face for any lingering sign of the seizure on the highway. “How’s the pressure?”

“Better,” Julian said, his voice level. He patted the edge of the mattress. “Sit down.”

Nadia hesitated for a fraction of a second, then walked over and sat down beside him. There was no awkwardness left between them—no ghosts of fifteen years ago, no lingering bitterness over choices made in another lifetime. When you are standing at the absolute edge of your own existence, social conventions and old pride strip away like dry paint, leaving only what is true.

“You shouldn’t have driven me down here,” Julian murmured, staring down at his hands. “You have a business, a home in Maine... you didn’t need to step back into the wreckage of my life.”

“Your life was never just yours, Julian,” Nadia replied gently. She reached over, her fingers brushing against his cold knuckles. “Your father made sure of that. And neither was mine, once.”

They sat in silence for a long time, listening to the steady hiss of car tires splashing through puddles on the highway outside.

In television dramas, the final hours of a dying man’s life were supposed to be filled with grand pronouncements, epic confrontations, or sweeping reconciliations under chandeliers. But reality was infinitely quieter, sharper, and more tender. A night without tomorrow wasn’t about breaking rules for the sake of chaos; it was about stripping away every lie you had ever told yourself about who you were supposed to be.

“I was so terrified of failing,” Julian whispered, the confession pulling free from a place very deep inside him. “I spent fifteen years building a career I didn’t even like, marrying a safety net, pretending I was the author of my own story, just because I was terrified that if I stopped running for one second, people would see I was ordinary. That I was just a frightened boy from a highway engineer’s house.”

Nadia leaned her head against his shoulder, her hair brushing his neck. “You were never ordinary, Julian. You were just terribly, stubbornly loyal to expectations that didn’t matter.”

“And Clara?” Julian’s voice cracked slightly. “What do I do about Clara? She’s hiding in that clinic in Boston right now, carrying a secret of her own. I came here to save her from my truth, and I find out she’s been drowning in her own dark water.”

“You go to her tomorrow,” Nadia said, lifting her head to look straight into his eyes. Her gaze was fierce, unyielding, and filled with a profound, unconditional grace. “Not as a savior, and not as a man running out the clock. You go to her as someone who finally knows what it means to be alive. You put your cards on the table—every single one of them—and you let her love you through the end of the page.”

Julian looked at her—at the woman who had walked out of his past only to anchor him in his final storm—and felt a strange, astonishing lightness wash over him.

The terror didn’t vanish entirely, but the grip it held on his chest broke. For the first time since walking out of Dr. Thorne’s office, Julian didn’t feel like a man serving a sentence. He felt like a man who, after a lifetime of wandering through a fog of obligations, had finally found his way back home.

They didn’t sleep that night. They sat side by side against the headboard as the rain drummed against the motel glass, talking through the hours—reclaiming old memories, laughing at the absurdities of their younger selves, and holding onto each other with the fierce, quiet urgency of two people watching the tide turn.

When the first pale, gray smear of dawn began to bleed through the Boston smog, Julian stood up, buttoned his coat, and picked up his phone.

The night without tomorrow was over. The page had turned. And the final chapter was waiting.

Chapter EighteenBefore I Die Wore

The Midpoint Twist

The Charles Street clinic. Two liars in a room full of recliners.

✦

The Charles Street clinic smelled of rubbing alcohol, weak tea, and the particular dry warmth of a building that had been overheated since October. Julian gave his name at the desk, and then, because the woman behind the glass was looking at him with patient expectation, he gave Clara’s too.

“Bay four,” the receptionist said. “Through the double doors, take a left at the fish tank.”

There was a fish tank. Julian walked past it without seeing a single fish. The infusion room beyond was a long, bright space lined with vinyl recliners, each one paired with a wheeled pole and a clear bag of something that looked like ordinary water and was not. A man in a Celtics cap slept with his mouth open. A woman in her thirties typed slowly on a laptop with one hand, the other tethered by tubing to her chest. A television on the wall played a morning show with the sound off.

Clara sat by the window in the fourth recliner, a gray blanket over her knees and a paperback face down on the armrest. She was wearing a soft cotton cap the color of oatmeal, and her cheekbones stood out in a way they had not at Sunday dinner three weeks ago, and her wrists looked very thin against the blanket.

She looked up.

For a moment neither of them spoke. Julian had rehearsed a hundred versions of this on the drive down—the accusation, the plea, the careful husbandly concern—and all of them evaporated. Clara’s expression was not guilt. It was the flat, exhausted calm of someone who had been carrying a heavy thing for a very long time and had just set it down.

“You weren’t supposed to see this yet,” she said.

Julian crossed the room and sat down on the stool beside her recliner. He took her hand. The back of it was bruised in a small purple flower around the IV port, and it was colder than it should have been.

“How long?” he asked.

“Since August.” Clara looked past him, out the window at the gray brick of the parking garage. “I found a lump under my arm in the shower the week the gallery closed for the remodel. I thought it was nothing. I thought it was a lymph node from a cold. I told myself that for eleven days.”

She turned her hand over and laced her fingers through his. “Hodgkin’s. Stage two. It’s the good kind, if there is such a thing. Twelve cycles. I’ve had eight. Dr. Okafor says the markers are falling faster than she expected. She used the word ‘excellent’ twice, which I’m told is unusual for her.”

Julian stared at her. “You’ve been driving into Boston twice a week for three months.”

“On Tuesdays and Fridays. I told you the restoration project had evening meetings.” Her mouth twisted. “The gallery closed on the twenty-eighth of August. I took the severance. I told you I was staying late because I couldn’t think of a better lie that fit the schedule.”

“Why?” His voice came out rougher than he meant it to. “Clara, why in God’s name wouldn’t you tell me?”

She was quiet for a moment. The television flickered silently above them.

“My mother died when I was nineteen,” Clara said. “You know that. What you don’t know is that I sat in a hospital corridor for four months and watched my father turn into a nurse, and then a widower, and then a man who flinched every time the phone rang. I swore I would never make anyone I loved do that for me.” She looked at him. “And you were working ninety hours a week, Julian. You came home at midnight and ate standing up over the sink. Every time I opened my mouth to tell you, I heard myself adding one more thing to the pile you were already carrying.”

“So you carried it alone.”

“So I carried it alone,” she agreed. “And then I found these.”

She reached into the canvas tote on the floor with her free hand and drew out a white envelope. Julian recognized it before she opened it—creased at one corner, the flap soft from being handled. Inside were Dr. Thorne’s pamphlets on palliative radiation, the ones he had shoved into the inside lining of his wool coat on the first afternoon and never properly hidden since.

“They were in the coat you hung behind the door,” Clara said. “I found them three weeks ago, the night before your mother’s Sunday dinner. I called Thorne the next morning. He wouldn’t tell me much—he’s a decent man—but he told me enough.”

The floor of the clinic seemed to tilt a few degrees.

“You knew,” Julian said. “At dinner. You knew, and you sat there and let me talk about beneficiary paperwork like a man reading a weather report.”

“My wine glass shook so badly I had to set it down,” Clara said. “You didn’t notice. You were too busy being noble.”

Julian started to speak and found that the words had gone somewhere else. He looked down at their joined hands—her bruised one and his cold one—and made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite anything else.

“We’re both liars,” he said.

“We’re both married,” Clara said. “It’s not the same thing. But it has been for a while.”

So he told her. All of it, sitting on a stool in bay four while a nurse changed the bag above Clara’s head and pretended not to listen: the diagnosis, the list of thirty-four truths, the attic strongbox, the wires to Marcus Vance, the train, Harbor Cove, Nadia, the shakedown that had built his career on his father’s pension. Clara listened without interrupting. When he got to the part about Nadia, her eyebrows lifted a fraction.

“Did you sleep with her?” she asked.

“No. God, no. Clara, she’s—”

“I wasn’t asking about fifteen years ago,” Clara said. “I was asking about last night.”

“No.” He met her eyes. “She drove me here and told me to put my cards on the table. That’s all she’s done.”

Clara studied him for a long moment, and then, unbelievably, she smiled—a small, tired, entirely real smile.

“Then I would like to meet her,” she said. “Any woman who can get Julian Mercer to drive to Maine and cry in a sail loft deserves a cup of hospital coffee.”

Nadia was in the corridor, leaning against the wall with two paper cups and the expression of someone who had already decided not to be offended by whatever happened next. Clara thanked her. Nadia said the coffee was terrible and that Clara should drink it anyway. The two of them looked at each other for a moment—fifteen years of history on one side, eleven years of marriage on the other—and then Clara said, “Thank you for bringing him back in one piece,” and Nadia said, “He was already in pieces. I just carried the box.”

Dr. Okafor came by at nine-thirty to look at the scan on her tablet and say the word “excellent” a third time. When she had gone, Julian sat with Clara until the bag was empty and the nurse came to unhook her.

“Six weeks,” Clara said quietly. It was not a question.

“Six to eight.”

She nodded, as though he had read out a test score. Then she swung her legs off the recliner, pulled her coat on over the blanket marks on her arms, and took his hand.

“Then we will be honest for six weeks,” she said. “All of it. No more protecting each other by disappearing.”

They walked out through the automatic doors into the cold Boston morning, two thin people holding each other up. It was not the end of the story. It was the middle—the place where, after years of separate silences, the two of them finally began telling the same one.

Act II · Part Two · Chapter NineteenBefore I Die Wore

The Airtight Trap

The twenty-fourth floor, a Saturday, and a folder with his signature on it.

✦

Marcus Vance had asked to meet on a Saturday, which meant the twenty-fourth floor was empty except for the cleaning crew and the low, conditioned hum of a building with no one in it.

Julian came up alone. The reception desk was dark. The glass conference room where he had burned his first mask three weeks earlier stood open and silent, its long walnut table wiped to a dull shine. Marcus was in his corner office, coat off, sleeves rolled, a glass of something amber beside a folder that had been squared precisely with the edge of his blotter.

Holt, the firm’s general counsel, sat in the chair by the window with a legal pad on his knee. He did not stand when Julian came in.

“Sit down, Julian,” Marcus said.

“I’ll stand.”

“Suit yourself.” Marcus opened the folder. Inside were perhaps forty pages, tabbed and numbered. “These are the 2018 Westchester corridor opinions. Zoning, right-of-way, the municipal acquisition memos. Your name is on eleven of them. Your bar number, your signature, your handwriting on the cover sheets.”

“I know what I signed.”

“Do you?” Marcus turned a page and rotated it toward him. “This one is dated the fourth of October. It certifies that the environmental impact review was complete and that all affected property owners had been notified. They hadn’t. Two of the parcels were still in probate. Arthur Henderson’s people knew, and you signed it anyway, because I put it on your desk at six o’clock on a Friday and told you it was routine.”

Julian looked at the page. He remembered the Friday. He remembered the stack of documents and the takeout container of cold lo mein and the ferry horn sounding somewhere out on the river.

“You told me it was routine,” he said.

“I did,” Marcus agreed pleasantly. “And a jury will ask why a senior associate at a top-ten firm signed a certification without reading it. And you will say, because my partner told me to. And they will write that down, and then they will look at the fact that your father’s account paid this firm’s holding company a hundred thousand dollars over four years, and they will draw a very old, very simple picture.”

Holt spoke without looking up from his pad. “The bar complaint would be filed by us, pre-emptively. It would name you as an unindicted co-conspirator and recommend disbarment. The criminal referral would follow within the week.”

“You’d burn your own firm,” Julian said.

“We’d burn one associate,” Marcus said. “The firm would cooperate fully, express its deep regret, and continue. That is what firms do.” He closed the folder. “Or. You sign a medical leave agreement. One point two million dollars, paid quarterly, no admission of anything. The firm covers your treatment, and Clara’s, and the mortgage on the brownstone. You resign quietly for health reasons. Everyone remembers you kindly. Your brother keeps his lease. Your wife keeps her reputation and her oncologist and her life.”

There it was—the trap, and the shape of it was beautiful in its way. Not a threat to kill him. A threat to make the little time he had left an ugly, public, litigated ruin for everyone he loved.

“How long have you been working on this?” Julian asked.

“Since you walked out of my briefing and turned off your phone.” Marcus picked up his glass. “I’ve had three weeks and a very good legal department. You’ve had a diagnosis and a bus ticket.”

Julian put his hands in his coat pockets. His right hand closed around his phone, thumb finding the record button by feel. He had checked the law on the drive down: New York was a one-party consent state.

“Then answer one question,” he said, “and I’ll sign whatever you put in front of me.”

Marcus raised an eyebrow.

“Why was my father paying you?”

The room went quiet. Holt’s pen stopped moving.

“Because I let him,” Marcus said.

“That isn’t an answer.”

“It is the only honest one I have.” Marcus set the glass down. “Your father came to this office in the spring of 2016 with a folder of bank statements and a question about a blind trust in Chicago. He was a highway engineer with a good pension, a dead wife, and a son who was about to be destroyed by a partner’s embezzlement. He asked me what it would cost to keep your name out of the federal audit.”

“And you told him.”

“I told him two thousand a month, for as long as the audit remained open.” Marcus shrugged. “He paid it for four years. He never missed a payment. Not once, not even the month he was in the hospital.”

Julian felt something cold and enormous settle in his chest. “You made him pay you for a crime you committed.”

“I made him pay me for a crime your partner committed,” Marcus said. “I merely kept the receipts. And before you decide to be righteous about it, ask yourself who your father was protecting. Not me. You.”

Holt looked up. “Marcus. That’s enough.”

“It’s a private conversation,” Marcus said. “He’s going to sign.”

Julian took his hand out of his pocket. The phone was still recording.

“No,” he said.

Marcus’s expression did not change, but something behind it closed like a door.

“You understand what happens Monday,” Holt said.

“I understand exactly what happens Monday.” Julian buttoned his coat. “You’ll file a complaint that admits, in writing, that a senior partner at this firm directed an associate to sign a false certification. You’ll explain to the ethics committee why the firm’s own holding company received a hundred thousand dollars from the father of the associate in question. And then you’ll explain it again to the Attorney General, and then to a reporter, and then to a jury.”

“You have six weeks,” Marcus said softly. “You’ll spend them in depositions.”

“I have six weeks,” Julian agreed, “and I intend to spend them loudly.”

He walked out through the empty reception, past the dark desk and the glass wall, and rode the elevator down twenty-four floors. In the lobby he stopped beneath the brass clock and played the recording back. Marcus’s voice came out of the little speaker, small and clear and damning: I told him two thousand a month, for as long as the audit remained open.

Julian stood there for a moment in the marble silence, holding the phone in both hands.

The first shot had not been fired. But the safety was off.

The First Shot

A diner near Grand Central, a folder of emails, and a story posted at six.

✦

At five, they met Moira Quinn in the back of a bar that smelled of bleach and old beer.

Moira was fifty, compact, with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and the flat, unhurried manner of someone who had been lied to professionally for twenty-five years. She listened to the recording twice. She read the ledger. She asked Julian three questions about dates and one about his father.

The Hardest Apology

He set the notebook down on the floor between them.

“I met you at a time in my life when everything I had built was shaking. My partner was gone, my license was in the air, my father was dying and I was too angry to sit with him. And you were—” He stopped. Started again. “You were the calmest person I had ever met. You didn’t need anything from me. You had your own work and your own money and your own opinions about paint colors, and you never once asked me to be more than I was. I told myself that was love. I think some of it was. But some of it was that I was terrified, and you looked like a place where nothing else could go wrong.”

Clara was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, her voice was steady and very careful.

“I know,” she said.

“You know.”

“Julian, I married you in a courthouse on a Tuesday with four people in the room and a judge who had a cold.” She turned to look at him. “I knew you were running from something. I thought if I stood still long enough, you’d eventually stop running and land next to me. And then eleven years went by, and you were still running, and I had gotten very good at being the place you came back to at midnight.”

“That isn’t an answer. That’s an absolution.”

“It’s both,” Clara said. “You don’t get to be the only one who was afraid. I married a man who was going somewhere because I wanted to go somewhere too. I just didn’t want to drive.”

Julian looked at her—at the cotton cap and the flour still under her thumbnail from the bread she had tried to make that afternoon and the small, determined set of her jaw—and felt something in his chest give way that had nothing to do with the tumor.

“I’m sorry,” he said. “Not for the diagnosis. Not for the lies about the pamphlets. For the eleven years. For every dinner I ate standing up over the sink. For every night you went to bed alone while I sat downstairs answering emails from a man who was stealing from my father. You deserved a husband who was actually in the room.”

Clara took his hand and pressed it flat against her cheek.

“I’m sorry too,” she said. “For the three months. For deciding that your dying meant I wasn’t allowed to be sick.” She closed her eyes. “We are both so stupid, Julian.”

“We are,” he agreed. “We’re going to have to be stupid together from now on.”

They sat on the kitchen floor until the tea went entirely cold and the radiator clicked off and the house settled into its deep, small hours. At some point Clara began to hum half a bar of the song she never finished, and Julian, who had heard it ten thousand times and never once listened, closed his eyes and listened.

The hardest apology was not the one that asked for forgiveness. It was the one that told the truth without asking for anything at all.

Chapter Twenty-ThreeBefore I Die Wore

Building Something That Lasts

Sea-glass green paint, a fund with his father’s name, and four to six weeks.

✦

The guest room had been taped off with blue painter’s strip for three years and four months.

Julian knew the exact figure because Clara had told him, twice a year, with increasing delicacy, that the room above the kitchen was the only part of the brownstone that still looked like a construction site. The walls were half-primed. The drop cloth had hardened into a shape like a dried riverbed. A ladder stood in the corner with a fan of paint chips on the top step.

On the Saturday after Clara’s tenth cycle, Daniel arrived with a van full of supplies he refused to itemize, and Evelyn arrived with a cooler of sandwiches nobody had asked for, and the four of them spent the day painting the guest room sea-glass green.

Julian lasted about ninety minutes on the roller before his arms began to shake. He did not argue about it. He sat on the floor with the trim brush and did the baseboards, which was slow, careful work that could be done sitting down, and which he could still do well.

“You’re cutting in like a man who’s never held a brush,” Daniel observed from the ladder.

“I’ve held a brush.”

“You’ve held a pen. It’s not the same.”

Evelyn, in the doorway with a sandwich in her hand, watched her two sons in the same room for the first time in four years and said nothing at all, which for Evelyn was a kind of speech.

By four o’clock the room was green and the windows were open despite the cold, and the four of them sat on the drop cloth eating turkey sandwiches and listening to the radiator tick. Clara had taken off her cap. Her scalp was coming back in a soft dark fuzz, fine as a duckling’s, and she kept touching it with the flat of her hand as though surprised.

“It’s a good room,” she said. “It gets the morning light.”

“It does,” Julian said.

He did not say what they were both thinking—that it would be his room by January, that the hospital bed would go where the drop cloth was, that the morning light would fall on the ceiling above it. There was no need. They had promised each other honesty, and honesty, he had discovered, did not always mean saying the terrible thing out loud. Sometimes it meant letting the other person know that you both already knew.

The fund was Daniel’s idea, though he would deny it later.

“You’ve got all this money coming at you,” he said that evening, when Evelyn had gone home and Clara was asleep upstairs. They were in the kitchen with the radio on low. “The firm’s settlement, the insurance, whatever the lawyers shake loose. What are you going to do with it?”

“Clara’s set for the mortgage and the treatment. There’s the pension, and the accounts.” Julian turned his mug around on the table. “The rest of it was going to be a very expensive headstone.”

“Then don’t.”

They talked until midnight. By the end of it they had a name, the Raymond Mercer Fund, and a purpose: legal aid and living expenses for whistleblowers—the people who found the thing and said it out loud and lost their jobs for it. Sarah Kim would run it. She had been suspended from the firm and had spent the last week on her sister’s couch in Queens, and when Julian called her at eleven-thirty at night to ask whether she wanted a job, she said, “What’s the salary?” and then, before he could answer, “I don’t care. Yes.”

The firm’s settlement offer—the one point two million, with the NDA—they refused in a two-line letter that Sarah wrote on his behalf and Clara read aloud twice because she liked the sound of it.

Dr. Thorne came to the house on the following Tuesday, which he did not normally do, and sat in the living room with his coat still on.

“The scan shows progression,” he said. “Not dramatically. But the margins have changed, and the swelling has increased, and I think we are now looking at four to six weeks rather than six to eight.”

Julian nodded. He had known before the doctor said it. He had known on the ladder, and on the floor with the trim brush, and in the kitchen with his brother.

“I understand.”

“There are options. There is a trial at Mass General that I could look into—”

“No,” Julian said, gently. “No more trials. I’d like to be awake for the rest of it.”

After Thorne left, Julian stood in the doorway of the green guest room and looked at the empty space where the bed would go, and at the morning light moving across the fresh paint. He could not build a future. That had been taken from him on a November afternoon in a room with a humming light, and no amount of bargaining would give it back.

But he could build a thing that would outlast him, and he could hand it to people who would keep it standing. A fund with his father’s name. A woman with a job she believed in. A room with sea-glass walls and a window that faced the sun.

It was not a future. It was a foundation. It would have to do.

Chapter Twenty-FourBefore I Die Wore

The Betrayal Exposed

A sealed envelope in a file box, and three years of silence.

✦

Holt resigned on the second Tuesday in December and spent the following morning in a conference room on the fourteenth floor of the Attorney General’s building, answering questions for six hours with a lawyer beside him and a cardboard box of files on the table.

The box was what Julian had been waiting for.

Moira Quinn called him at two in the afternoon. “The general counsel is cooperating. He’s given the investigators Marcus’s personal file room—the things that never went into the firm’s system. There’s a box with your father’s name on it.”

Julian sat down on the stairs. “What’s in it?”

“I don’t know. They won’t let me see it yet. But I thought you should hear it from me before you heard it from a reporter on your lawn.”

The box arrived at the brownstone three days later, delivered by a paralegal from the Attorney General’s office who apologized for the hour and would not come inside. It was a standard archive carton, the kind Julian had once used for case files, with a strip of masking tape on the side and three words in Marcus Vance’s small, precise handwriting: MERCER, R. — PERSONAL.

Clara sat beside him on the living-room rug while he opened it.

Inside were copies of the wire receipts. A photograph of the Westchester parcels. A memo from 2018 in Marcus’s hand recommending that “the junior associate be kept in the dark as to the probate status of parcels 7 and 9.” And, at the bottom, beneath a folder of bank correspondence, a single envelope, sealed, yellowed at the flap, with his father’s block printing on the front.

For Julian. When he is ready.

Julian held it for a long moment without opening it. The paper was thin and dry under his thumb. His father had written those words in 2020, in the last months of his life, and Marcus Vance had kept the envelope in a box for three years because a sealed letter from a dead man was a useful thing to own.

“He never gave it to me,” Julian said. “He had it the whole time. At the funeral. At the probate hearing. Every time he shook my hand in that office and told me I was going to make senior partner.”

Clara put her hand on his back and did not say anything.

He opened the envelope.

Julian,

If you are reading this, then I am gone, and Marcus has finally done the decent thing, which I do not expect. I am writing it anyway. Your mother always said I was an optimist about the wrong people.

You will have found the ledger by now. You will have done the arithmetic and concluded that your father was a fool and a coward. Both may be true. But I want you to understand what I was buying, because it was never silence.

In 2016 a man from the firm came to the house and told me that a federal audit was going to name you as a party to your partner’s crimes. He said it could be settled quietly. He said it would cost two thousand a month, and I said, “For how long?” and he said, “For as long as I say.” I paid him for four years because I did not know how to tell my son that the life he was so proud of had been built by a thief, and I was afraid that if you found out, you would believe it was your fault.

It was not your fault. It was never your fault. You signed what you were told to sign, the way I signed the state’s road surveys for thirty-five years, because a man on the other side of the desk said it was routine. That is not a crime. It is a habit, and it is the habit of every decent man who has ever worked for someone else.

Julian read it twice. The second time, Clara read it over his shoulder, and when she reached the line about the popsicle bridge she made a small sound and pressed her forehead against his arm.

The exposure that followed was not dramatic. It was bureaucratic and total, which was worse for Marcus and better for everyone else. The State Bar filed formal disciplinary charges on the eighteenth of December. The Attorney General’s office executed a search warrant at Vance & Sterling on the nineteenth, and two associates carried boxes out of the twenty-fourth floor past a row of paralegals taking photographs on their phones. Holt’s testimony—along with the sealed envelope, the ledger, Sarah’s emails, and the recording of Marcus saying two thousand a month, for as long as the audit remained open—was enough to convince a grand jury before Christmas.

Marcus Vance was suspended from the practice of law on the twenty-first. He was indicted on the ninth of January on charges of wire fraud, obstruction, and conspiracy to defraud the state of New York.

Julian watched the arraignment on the little television in the green guest room, propped up on pillows, with Clara asleep in the chair beside him. Marcus came down the courthouse steps in a gray coat with his hands cuffed in front of him. He looked smaller than he had in the corner office. He looked like a man who had spent his entire life being the most important person in the room and had just discovered what the room looked like from outside.

Julian turned the television off.

“Well?” Clara murmured, without opening her eyes.

He thought about it for a moment.

“I thought I’d feel something,” he said. “I don’t.”

“What do you feel?”

Julian looked down at the letter in his lap, folded now along its old creases, and at the green walls his brother had painted, and at his wife asleep in the chair with her hand still resting on the blanket where his foot was.

“Tired,” he said. “And lucky.”

Chapter Twenty-FiveBefore I Die Wore

Falling Apart

A seizure in the hallway, a fight at three in the morning, and the bell Clara rang alone.

✦

The first bad one came on a Wednesday morning in the upstairs hallway, halfway between the bedroom and the bathroom, with no warning at all.

Julian remembered the carpet coming up to meet him. He remembered Clara’s voice saying his name from a long way away, and the sound of something heavy falling—the hall table, he learned later, and the brass bowl of keys that had been on it since they moved in. He did not remember the ambulance, or the paramedics, or the eight minutes Clara spent on her knees beside him with her hand under his head, counting his breaths and telling him, over and over, that she was there.

He woke up in a white room with a plastic rail on the bed and a neurologist named Dr. Feld explaining that the tumor had pressed against a new piece of real estate overnight.

“The seizures are likely to recur,” she said. “We can manage them, but you need to stop driving, and you should not be alone in the house during the day.”

“I’m dying, doctor,” Julian said. “I was rather hoping to be alone at some point.”

The joke landed badly. Feld looked at her hands, and Clara looked at the wall, and Julian understood that he had crossed a line he had not known was there: the line between a man who could still be wry about it and a man who needed to be taken seriously.

He was in the hospital for four days. On the second day, Clara had her twelfth and final chemotherapy cycle at the Charles Street clinic, and Julian watched it on Daniel’s phone: Clara in bay four, hooked up to the last bag, wearing the oatmeal cap, ringing the little brass bell above the recliner while the nurses clapped. Daniel had filmed it sideways, and the sound was bad, and Clara was crying and laughing at the same time and saying, “I’m done, I’m done,” to no one in particular.

Julian watched it eleven times.

On the fourth day they let him come home. The green room was finished. There was a hospital bed in it now, with a rail and a pressure mattress and a chair beside it, and Clara had hung a paper garland across the window because she said the room needed something that wasn’t beige.

That was the week the falling apart began in earnest—not the dramatic collapse he had imagined in November, but a hundred small surrenders. He could no longer read more than a paragraph without losing the thread. His right hand had developed a fine, constant tremor that made his fountain pen useless; he switched to a pencil, and then, on the days when even the pencil was too much, to Clara writing down what he said. He slept fourteen hours a day. He forgot the name of the fund’s accountant twice and Sarah twice more, and each time the forgetting was worse than the headache.

The legal machinery did not pause to be kind. Marcus’s defense team subpoenaed Clara’s medical records to argue that Julian’s accusations were the product of a dying man’s confusion. They subpoenaed Sarah for every email she had ever sent. And on the twenty-eighth of December, the firm’s insurers froze the settlement escrow, which meant the Raymond Mercer Fund had a name, a board, a director, and no money.

Julian took the call from Sarah in the green room with the phone on speaker because he could no longer reliably hold it to his ear.

“We’ll get it back,” Sarah said. “It’s a delay, not a death.” A pause. “Sorry.”

“Don’t apologize. It’s a delay, not a death. I’ll put it on a mug.”

He hung up and lay back against the pillows and looked at the garland, and something in him—something that had been holding very still since November—finally gave way.

When Clara came in with his evening pills, he would not take them.

“Julian.”

“Leave them.”

“You have to take them.”

“I have to do nothing.” His voice came out ugly, scraped raw. “I have to lie here and be fed and watched and written down. I have to listen to my brother talk about paint. I have to have my wife bathe me. Do not tell me what I have to do.”

Clara stood very still with the little paper cup in her hand.

“I rang the bell alone,” she said. “Did you know that? Twelve cycles, and I rang the bell alone.”

“I was in a hospital bed.”

“I know where you were. I’m not blaming you. I’m telling you that I am tired too.” Her voice shook, and she did not stop. “I am tired of being brave in a house where the bravest thing I can do is pretend I’m not angry. I am angry, Julian. I am angry at you for dying. I am angry at the tumor and at Marcus and at your father for paying him and at my own body for being the thing that got better. Do you understand? I am going to live, and you are not, and I have to be grateful for it in front of you every single day.”

The room went very quiet.

Julian looked at her—at the paper cup in her hand and the ring of white where her fingers were pressing it too hard—and felt the ugly thing in his chest turn over and show him its underside.

“I’m sorry,” he said.

“I don’t want an apology.” Clara sat down on the edge of the bed. “I want you to be angry with me instead of at me.” She set the cup on the nightstand. “We have four weeks, maybe less, and I refuse to spend them being polite.”

They sat in the dark for a long time. Then Julian reached out—with the hand that still worked—and took the little paper cup, and swallowed the pills, and said, “They taste like chalk and regret.”

“I know,” Clara said. “I’ve been swallowing them for four months.”

She lay down beside him on the narrow hospital bed, and he put his arm across her, and they stayed like that until the morning light came through the window under the paper garland and made a pattern of small dark birds on the sea-glass wall.

Act III · Chapter Twenty-SixBefore I Die Wore

The Circle Grows Small

Hospice, a nurse named Bea, and the people who fit in one room.

✦

The hospice nurse arrived on the second of January with a canvas bag, a laminated folder, and the calm, unbureaucratic manner of a woman who had done this several hundred times and had never once been in a hurry about it.

Her name was Beatrice Osei, and she told them to call her Bea. She was sixty-one, built like a retired weightlifter, and she had a laugh that started somewhere in her shoes.

“Right,” she said, sitting down at the kitchen table and opening the folder. “Here’s what happens. You’ll sleep more. Then you’ll sleep most of the day. Then you’ll stop wanting food, and people will try to make you eat, and I’ll tell them to stop. There’ll be some confusion, some restlessness, maybe some trouble swallowing. At the end there’ll be a change in your breathing, and it will sound worse than it feels.” She looked at Julian over her glasses. “You won’t be frightened. Other people will be frightened for you. That’s the hard part, and it isn’t yours.”

Julian found himself smiling. “You don’t soften things.”

Julian lay in the green room on the last night of January and listened to the house settling around him, and thought that the circle had not really grown small at all. It had finally grown to the right size.

Chapter Twenty-SevenBefore I Die Wore

The Final Harbor

Snow on the pier, a girl with a wooden boat, and a photograph come true.

✦

Dr. Feld said no for four minutes and then, because Julian had already stopped listening, said, “Fine. But you take the oxygen, you stop every two hours, and if you have another seizure on the road, you turn around.”

They left on a Friday morning in the first week of February, in Daniel’s pickup, because the pickup had a bench seat and Julian could lie against Clara’s shoulder with a blanket over his knees and a portable oxygen concentrator humming on the floor. Daniel drove. Clara sat in the middle. Evelyn followed in her own car with the cooler and the medication bag, because nobody in the history of the Mercer family had ever gone anywhere without a cooler.

The drive north took twice as long as it should have. They stopped in Portsmouth for soup, and again outside Portland so Julian could stand in the cold and look at the trees, and once more on the shoulder of Route 1 because Clara had begun to cry and needed to stop, and Julian held her hand and said nothing at all.

Harbor Cove in February was a different town from the one he had walked into in November. The fog was gone. The sky was a hard, brilliant blue, and the water in the cove was the color of gunmetal, and there was snow on the roofs of the lobster shacks and snow on the stacked traps and snow in the branches of the spruce grove behind Nadia’s loft. “You look like a ghost that got dressed in the dark,” she said.

“Because I have.” She put an arm around him and helped him down. “Come inside. Wren’s been baking since six and the kitchen is a disaster.”

She was the child on the pier. She was six years old and laughing into the sun beside his father, and now she was standing at the stove in an apron that said Don’t Ask Me About The Boat, and she turned around and looked at him and said, “Oh. You’re him.”

“I’m him.”

“Uncle Ray talked about you all the time.” Wren set down the spoon. “I’m sorry. Mom said you were coming. I made cinnamon rolls because it seemed like the thing to do and now there are forty of them.”

They sat in the kitchen for the afternoon. Nadia explained it, because Julian was too tired to piece it together himself: she had worked at the bank in Boston that processed the First Merchants wires, as a paralegal in the compliance office, until she left in 2015. His father had found her through a cousin and come to her in 2016 with a folder of statements. She had told him what the routing codes meant. She had told him not to pay. He had paid anyway, and come back twice a year, and sat on the porch and asked the same questions, and she had made him tea and told him the same answers.

“He used to bring Wren wooden things,” Nadia said. “A boat with a sail. A little house with a door that opened. He said his older son had built a bridge out of popsicle sticks when he was nine, and he had been trying to build something as good ever since.”

Wren went out to the workshop and came back with a wooden sailboat, maybe eight inches long, its mast a dowel, its sail a scrap of canvas, its hull sanded smooth and oiled to a warm honey color.

“He made it the year he died,” she said. “I was eight. He said to keep it until I met you, and then to decide whether to give it to you.”

Julian held the boat in both hands. It was lighter than it looked. The little door in the hull opened on a brass pin, and inside there was a folded square of paper with his father’s block printing on it: For J. — It floats.

He laughed, and then he put his forehead down on the table and wept, and Clara sat beside him with her hand on his back, and no one in the kitchen said a word.

In the afternoon they walked out to the pier. It took twenty minutes to go fifty yards, with Daniel on one side and Clara on the other and the oxygen humming, but they got there, and the pier was snow-covered and the boats were mostly out of the water and the lighthouse on the headland stood against the blue with the white spray cutting up against the rocks.

Julian took out the faded Polaroid and held it up against the harbor and matched the curve of the seawall and the tilted red cannery shed, just as he had in November. The photograph was true—it had been true all along; he had simply been looking at the wrong part of it.

“Item fourteen,” he said.

Clara turned her face up to him. “What?”

“Take Clara back to the coast in Maine, out of season, when the wind is cold.” He folded the photograph back into his coat. “Done.”

That night they ate cinnamon rolls and chowder in the loft with the stove ticking and the wind off the marsh rattling the corrugated roof. Julian fell asleep at the table with his head on his arms, and the four of them—his wife, his brother, and the two women of the loft—sat up and talked in low voices while the dark came down.

He had come north in November to find a ghost. He had found a family instead.

Chapter Twenty-EightBefore I Die Wore

The Threshold

The ambulance from Harbor Cove, and a conversation on a pier that wasn’t there.

✦

It happened at two in the morning, in the loft, on the second night.

Julian woke with a taste like pennies in his mouth and a pressure behind his left eye that was not pain but something larger than pain, something with a shape. He tried to say Clara’s name. What came out was a sound, and then the room folded in half.

He heard the kitchen chair go over. He heard Nadia on the stairs, and Clara’s voice sharp with terror, and Daniel saying call 911, call 911 in the flat, controlled voice of a man who had once been a volunteer firefighter and had never quite stopped being one. He felt the cold floor under his cheek. He felt someone’s fingers in his hair.

Then he was not in the loft anymore.

He was standing on the pier at Harbor Cove in the middle of a summer afternoon, and the water was that impossible flat cerulean from the print in Dr. Thorne’s office, and the light was the light of a day that had never happened.

His father sat on the end of the pier in a canvas fishing jacket, mending a net.

“You’re late,” Raymond Mercer said, without looking up.

Julian sat down beside him. The wood of the pier was warm through his trousers. He was not tired. He was not wearing a hospital gown or an oxygen tube. He was thirty-eight years old and his hands were steady and the whole enormous blue day stretched out in front of him without a single appointment in it.

“Am I dead?” he asked.

“You’re at a threshold.” His father pulled a knot tight. “It’s a word I learned from a nurse. It means the place where you decide.”

The Climax

The eleventh of March, a verdict at four-forty, and a letter with no stamp.

✦

Evelyn sat in the chair with her handbag on her lap as though she were waiting for a train. Sarah was on speakerphone from the courthouse steps, and Nadia was on the other phone from the loft in Maine, and the laptop was propped on the cedar chest, and the whole small country of his life was in the room.

What he felt instead was a great, quiet exhaustion, and underneath it something he had not anticipated at all: sorrow. Marcus Vance had been his mentor for eleven years. He had taught Julian how to write a brief and how to order wine and how to stand in a courtroom with his hands still. He had also been a thief and an extortionist and the man who had kept a dead man’s letter in a box for three years. Both things were true. Before I Die

The lighthouse at dawn, a brass compass, and the last blank line.

✦

They left for Maine on a Thursday morning in the second week of April, with Bea in the back seat between the oxygen concentrator and a cooler of medication, and Daniel driving very slowly, and Clara in the middle with Julian’s head against her shoulder.

“Item thirty,” Julian said. “See the lighthouse one more time.” He watched it for a while. “Done.”

Then he reached into the pocket of his coat and took out the small brass compass his father had given him twenty years ago, on the day he made junior partner. The glass was scratched. The needle still held north.

“This was Dad’s,” he said. “He gave it to me when I got the promotion. I thought it was a joke about how I’d never find my way home.” He turned it over in his palm. “I want Wren to have it.”

Clara looked at him. “Are you sure?”

“She’s the one who’s going to build things that float.” He held it out. “Wren. Come here.”

Wren came down the pier with her hands in her jacket pockets. Julian put the compass into her palm and closed her fingers around it.

“It doesn’t point to anything but north,” he said. “That’s the whole trick. You still have to decide which way to walk.”

Wren looked at the compass for a moment. Then she leaned down and kissed his cheek, and went back up the pier very fast, and Nadia put an arm around her shoulders.

The sun was fully up now. The fishing boats were going out past the breakwater, one after another, their wakes laying white lines across the blue.

“Thirty-four,” Clara said quietly.

Julian looked at her. In the clear morning light she was thin and tired and her hair was coming back in a soft dark fuzz, and she was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen in his life.

“Tell the truth out loud to the person I love, with nothing left to hide,” he said.

“You’ve done that.”

“Not all of it.”

He took her hand—the good hand, the one that still worked—and held it in both of his.

“I’m afraid,” he said. “I’ve been afraid since November and I’ve been managing it and joking about it and being brave in front of everybody, and the truth is that I am afraid of the dark and I am afraid of the pain and I am afraid of the moment when you are in this world and I am not. That’s the last thing I was hiding.”

Clara’s eyes filled. She did not look away.

“I know,” she said. “I’ve known the whole time.”

“And the other thing.” His voice cracked. “I want you to live, Clara. Not the way people say it at funerals. I want you to have a studio with north light and a dog and a boat you can actually sail, and I want you to be happy in a way that has nothing to do with me. I want you to be loved again, by somebody who is in the room. Don’t spend the rest of your life being the widow of a man who died in November.”

Clara put her forehead against his.

“I’m going to miss you every day,” she said. “That’s not the same as not living.”

“No,” he said. “It isn’t.”

They sat on the bench while the harbor woke up around them. A truck pulled into the wharf and a man in oilskins began loading traps. A dog barked somewhere behind the cannery. The lighthouse light went round and round, pale and unnecessary in the daylight, faithful as a heartbeat.

Julian took the linen notebook out of his coat and opened it to the last page. He could not write, so Clara held the pen, and he said, “Write this at the bottom. Thirty-four. Tell the truth out loud. Done.”

She wrote it. Then she drew the pen through the last blank line, and closed the notebook, and put it in his hands.

“Take me home,” Julian said. “I’m tired.”

They took him back to the loft and put him to bed in the downstairs room with the window open onto the marsh. Bea gave him something for the pain. Daniel sat with him for a while and told him about the hinge on the cedar chest, which he had finally fixed, and how it had taken him three tries and a trip to the hardware store in Ellsworth. Evelyn came in and did not do the crossword; she just held his hand. Nadia looked in at the door and said, “The tide’s coming in,” and Julian said, “Good,” and closed his eyes.

At some point in the afternoon the light on the wall turned gold, and then amber, and then the particular blue that comes before the dark. Clara lay beside him with her head on his chest. The house was very quiet. Out on the marsh a bird called twice and stopped.

“Clara,” he said.

“I’m here.”

“I finished the list.”

“You finished the list.”

He was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, very softly, “Every mask has an ending,” and Clara said, “Yes,” and held him, and the tide came in across the marsh in the dark, and Julian Mercer stopped breathing at nineteen minutes past eight on a Friday evening in April, with nothing left to hide.

Epilogue

One year later. The bench, the fund, and a boat that floats.

✦

In April of the following year, Clara Mercer drove north to Harbor Cove with a dog in the back seat and a duffel bag in the trunk and the windows down for the first time since October.

The dog was a black retriever mix named Pilot, adopted in February from a shelter in Portland. The duffel contained three days of clothes, a folder of fund correspondence, and a linen notebook that she had not opened in eleven months.

The town looked the way it always looked in April: mud and daffodils and boats coming out of the shed. Nadia was on the porch with a mug of tea when Clara pulled in. Wren was at the kitchen table with a textbook and a laptop and a bowl of cereal, home for spring break, her hair shorter than it had been and her dorm key on a lanyard around her neck.

“You brought the dog,” Wren said.

“I brought the dog.”

“Mom said you wouldn’t.”

“Your mother says a lot of things.”

They walked down to the pier in the afternoon, the three of them and the dog, and sat on the bench with the brass plaque. The harbor was full of water and light. The lighthouse turned at the end of the point. Clara took the linen notebook out of her bag and opened it to the last page, where thirty-four lines had been crossed through in fountain pen, and a thirty-fifth line had been added in her own handwriting at the bottom, in pencil, in the weeks after the funeral: Keep the room green.

The Raymond Mercer Fund had thirty-one open cases and two full-time lawyers and an office above a bakery in Albany. Sarah Kim ran it with a ferocity that had already cost her two negotiations and won four. The bridge over the Hudson had been inspected twice that year, and Peter Vasquez had been promoted to crew chief, and his daughter had written a school essay about him that Sarah kept in her desk drawer.

Daniel had sold the hardware store in the fall and stayed on three days a week behind the counter because he liked it. He and Marisol had bought a house on Cedar Avenue with a bad roof and a good garden. Evelyn had moved into the downstairs bedroom and taken over the tomato plants, which had survived the move in the back of Daniel’s truck and produced, in her words, “a frankly obscene quantity of tomatoes.”

Marcus Vance was serving eleven years at a federal facility in Pennsylvania. He had written to Clara twice. She had not answered either letter, and she had not thrown them away. They were in a shoebox in the attic, above the green room, and she had decided that she was allowed not to know yet what they were for.

Clara had reopened a small restoration studio in Portland in September, with north light and a lease she could afford and a sign on the door that said MERCER & CO. She had hired an apprentice named Ruthie who was twenty-four and fast with a scalpel and thought Clara was the most interesting person she had ever met. On Fridays, Clara taught a class at the art school. On Sundays, she sailed with a widower named Tom who had a badly maintained sloop and a very good laugh, and who was, as Nadia had predicted, entirely uncomplicated.

“Are you going to open it?” Wren asked, nodding at the notebook.

Clara looked at the list for a while. Thirty-four lines. A man who had walked out of a law firm in November with six weeks to live and had spent every one of them telling the truth.

“I already know what it says,” she said. She closed the notebook. “I wanted to bring it, that’s all. I wanted him to see the water.”

They sat on the bench until the sun went down. Out past the breakwater, a small white sail was coming in on the evening tide, and Clara watched it until it turned toward the mooring field and furled its canvas and disappeared into the gray.

“It floats,” Wren said.

Clara put her hand on the brass plaque, warm from the afternoon, and did not say anything at all.

Author’s Note

On lists, and the people who keep them.

Before I Die Wore began with a question I could not answer: what would a man do if he found out that the life he had built was a mask, and that he had only a few weeks left to take it off?

Julian Mercer is not a portrait of anyone I know. But the ledger in his father’s strongbox is a real kind of document, and the arrangements it records—the quiet monthly payments, the careful routing, the shame that keeps a family from asking—are real too. Most of the harm in this book is done by people who believe they are protecting someone. That is the mask I wanted to write about: the one that looks like love from the inside.

The list of thirty-four things came first, before the chapters. I wrote it on a train between New York and Boston and then spent a year working out who would need to write each line, and why. Some of the items are small on purpose. A dying man does not only want to see the pyramids; he wants to finish the guest room. He wants to tell his brother he was wrong. He wants to stop apologizing for taking up space.

If this book has a wish, it is not that you make a list. It is that you read the page in front of you honestly, and tell the people you love what they are, while there is still time to hear it said out loud.

Amim Ahsan
Dhaka, 2026
