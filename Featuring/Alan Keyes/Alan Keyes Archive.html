Overture

Why keep a file on a man who lost?

Because elections are remembered by their winners, and arguments are remembered by their losers. This archive keeps the losing argument intact.

Alan Keyes is the rare figure in American politics whose candidacy is easier to describe than to explain. He held no office. He never won a statewide race. He ran for president three times and for the Senate four, and lost every time. And yet, for a season in 1999 and 2000, he was one of the most quotable, most watched, and most disruptive speakers in the Republican Party — a man with a doctorate from Harvard and a voice built for the back of a hall.

What follows is not a verdict. It is a file: the record of the man, the campaign he built around a single moral argument, the night in Iowa when the argument briefly beat the arithmetic, and the document that this archive was assembled around — a scanned dossier of the 2000 campaign, kept here in full.

Readers who came for the speech will find the speech. Readers who came for the numbers will find those too, including the ones that ended the campaign. The two belong in the same folder. That is the point of an archive.

Record

A Life Before the Campaign

The résumé matters here, because the campaign was an argument about consistency — and consistency is a claim about a life.

  1. 1950

    Born in Baltimore, Maryland

    Alan Lee Keyes, the fifth of eleven children, in a family that placed argument and church at the centre of the table. He would spend his career treating both as public vocations.

  2. 1979

    Into the Foreign Service

    After Harvard — undergraduate, then a doctorate in government — Keyes joined the U.S. Foreign Service, serving at the consulate in Mumbai and later at the embassy in Zimbabwe.

  3. 1985–1987

    Reagan’s diplomat

    Appointed Ambassador to the United Nations Economic and Social Council, then Assistant Secretary of State for International Organization Affairs. This is the credential he would carry into every later debate.

  4. 1988 · 1992

    Two runs for the Senate in Maryland

    Both lost, both by wide margins, both conducted as moral campaigns rather than managerial ones. The pattern that defined 2000 was already set.

  5. 1996

    The first presidential run

    Keyes entered the Republican primaries and became, for many viewers, the debate’s conscience and its irritant — pressing every rival on abortion and refusing to accept the moderators’ clock.

  6. June – Sept 1999

    Keyes 2000

    An exploratory committee on June 17, a formal announcement on September 21 in Bedford, New Hampshire. The slogan was “Keyes to the White House.”

  7. 2004 · 2008

    After the campaign

    A Senate run in Illinois against a young state senator named Barack Obama, and a final presidential campaign in 2008. Neither altered the record of 2000; both extended it.

The 2000 Campaign

Six Chapters of an Improbable Season

I

The Announcement

He declared in New Hampshire, in the state that had made and broken so many insurgents. The setting was deliberate: not a ballroom, not a convention floor, but the small room where the argument would have to be won first. From the beginning the campaign was organised around a claim that the Republican Party had learned to talk about principles without practising them, and that he — a diplomat, a father of three, a convert to the cause long before it was convenient — was the proof that the words still had weight.

II

The Iowa Surprise

On January 24, 2000, with George W. Bush at 41 percent and Steve Forbes at 30, Alan Keyes finished third with roughly 14 percent of the Iowa caucus vote — ahead of two sitting United States senators and every other conservative in the field. He had not climbed out of single digits in the pre-caucus polls. The result was treated, briefly, as one of the night’s genuine surprises: the orator had out-performed the organisers.

III

The Debates

Iowa bought him a seat at the table. In the nationally televised debates that followed, Keyes did what he had done in 1996: he treated the format as a moral examination rather than a forum for position papers, and he treated his rivals as witnesses who could be cross-examined. He stayed in the race after the early states had effectively decided it, debating Bush and McCain into the spring — a candidacy conducted, in his own framing, for the record rather than for the nomination.

IV

The Message

The platform was consistent to the point of stubbornness: opposition to abortion as the defining human-rights question of the age; support for tax reform and a flatter, simpler code; school choice; the defence of marriage as a public institution; and a foreign policy argued from first principles rather than from the polling of allies. He spoke of the family as the first government. He spoke of America as a proposition rather than a place. The message did not change with the audience, which is why the audiences, when they came, came for the whole of it.

V

The Arithmetic

A campaign is a message plus a machine, and the machine was never built. Roughly fifteen million dollars raised against a front-runner with a national apparatus; no statewide organisation; no delegate strategy that survived the first tier of primaries. Keyes could fill a room and could not fill a ballot line. By March the nomination was Bush’s in all but name, and the question was no longer whether Keyes could win but what his staying in the race was for.

VI

The Withdrawal

He withdrew on July 25, 2000, months after the race had been settled, and endorsed the nominee he had spent a year criticising. That ending is the honest one: the campaign was never a bid for the machinery of the party. It was a bid for its conscience, filed publicly, in the hope that the file would outlast the season. This archive exists on the same bet.
